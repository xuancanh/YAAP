# When to get help now

This doc exists so the agent never treats an urgent situation as a logging task. It doesn't help decide what's wrong; it helps decide **who to call, now**. When in doubt, the answer is always: contact a medical professional. Parents' instincts count: "I'm worried" is reason enough to call.

Guidance below is quoted from official sources (UK NHS and US American Academy of Pediatrics), checked November 2026. Other countries have their own urgent-advice numbers; find the family's and keep it in their file.

## Call emergency services (999 in the UK, 911 in the US)

The NHS lists these signs in a child with a high temperature as needing an emergency call:

- Stiff neck
- A rash that doesn't fade when a glass is pressed against it
- Bothered by light
- A fit (seizure) for the first time, or one that doesn't stop
- Unusually cold hands and feet
- Blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue
- Drowsy and hard to wake
- Extremely agitated, crying constantly, or confused
- Difficulty breathing, breathlessness or very fast breathing
- Not responding normally or not interested in feeding

Also call emergency services for any baby who has stopped breathing, is choking and can't breathe, or is unresponsive.

## Get urgent medical advice the same day

**Fever in young babies is the clearest rule both agencies give:**

| Source | Baby's age | Temperature | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| NHS | Under 3 months | 38°C or higher (or you think they have a high temperature) | Call 111 or GP |
| NHS | 3–6 months | 39°C or higher | Call 111 or GP |
| AAP | Under 3 months (12 weeks) | 100.4°F (38.0°C) or higher | Call the doctor right away |

The NHS also lists: signs of dehydration (few wet nappies, sunken eyes, no tears), a high temperature with a rash, a fever lasting five days or more, and not feeding or not their usual self when the parent is worried.

The agent's job: say "That needs a call now — <number>", then offer to note the time and readings for the call. Don't suggest home treatment or doses.

## Parent wellbeing

Low mood, anxiety, intrusive or scary thoughts, and feeling unable to cope are common after birth and treatable. Respond warmly, take it seriously, and share real resources:

- **US:** Postpartum Support International helpline 1-800-944-4773. For a crisis, call or text **988**.
- **UK:** talk to the GP, midwife or health visitor; NHS 111; Samaritans on 116 123. In immediate danger, 999.
- **Elsewhere:** the national crisis line and the family's doctor.

If the parent mentions thoughts of harming themselves or the baby, or says they're not safe: give the emergency number first, gently encourage them to call now or to get another adult with them, and stay with them in the conversation. Do not file it as a task.

## What to log after (not before) getting help

- Time symptoms started, temperature readings with times and method
- Feeds and wet nappies in the last 24 hours (from the [log](log-template.md))
- What the clinician said and any follow-up instructions, in their words

## Common mistakes

- Offering reassurance ("it's probably nothing").
- Looking for patterns in the log while the parent is worried about now.
- Burying the emergency number under logistics.
