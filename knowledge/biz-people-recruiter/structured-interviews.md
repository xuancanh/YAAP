# Structured interviews

A structured interview asks every candidate the same questions, tied to the competencies the job needs, and scores answers against the same defined scale. The US Office of Personnel Management's guidance describes this approach and notes that limiting interviewer discretion in how answers are elicited and rated improves agreement between interviewers. In practice it also makes decisions easier to explain.

## Two question types

**Behavioural** (past): "Tell me about a time you…". Best when candidates are likely to have relevant experience.

> "Tell me about a time a support queue fell behind its response target. What did you do, and what happened?"

**Situational** (hypothetical): "What would you do if…". Useful when candidates may not have done the exact thing before.

> "A large customer threatens to cancel unless you give them a refund our policy doesn't allow. Walk me through how you'd handle it."

Write one or two main questions per competency, each with a few follow-up probes:
- "What exactly did *you* do?" (separates the candidate from the team)
- "How did you know it worked?"
- "What would you do differently?"

Ask every candidate the main questions in the same order. Probes can vary with the answer, but stick to the competency.

## Anchored rating scale

A number means nothing without anchors. For each competency, write what an answer at each level sounds like. Use at least three defined levels; five is common.

**Example: queue management**

| Score | Anchor |
|---|---|
| 1 | No relevant example, or describes effort without a method ("we all worked late") |
| 2 | Example with some method, but vague on their own role or on results |
| 3 | Clear example: identified the cause, changed staffing or priorities, result described |
| 4 | As 3, plus measured the result and adjusted when the first change didn't fully work |
| 5 | As 4, plus made the change stick (a process, a forecast, coaching others) and can explain trade-offs |

Write anchors before interviews start and keep them with the questions.

## Interviewer brief

Each interviewer gets: the competencies they own (one or two), their questions and probes, the anchors, the candidate's CV, and these rules:

- Take notes on what the candidate said, close to verbatim, not on your impression.
- Score each competency alone, against the anchors, straight after the interview.
- Submit your scorecard before you talk to anyone else about the candidate.
- Don't assess competencies you weren't assigned. If you saw something important, note it as an observation.
- Leave time for the candidate's questions.

## Running the debrief

1. Confirm all scorecards are in. No debrief with missing scorecards.
2. Go competency by competency, not interviewer by interviewer. For each: scores, then the evidence.
3. Where scores differ a lot, ask each interviewer for the specific evidence. Often one heard something the other didn't.
4. The hiring manager decides, with the reasons tied to the scorecard. Record the decision and reasons.
5. Discuss process issues (a question that didn't work) separately, after the decision.

## Biases to watch for

- **Halo/horns:** one strong or weak moment colours everything. Counter: score competencies separately.
- **Similarity:** preferring people like us (same background, same interests). Counter: anchors and evidence.
- **First impressions:** deciding in the first minutes. Counter: score after all questions.
- **Contrast:** rating against the previous candidate. Counter: rate against anchors.
- **Groupthink:** the most senior voice sets the tone. Counter: independent scorecards; the most junior speaks first.

## Work samples

For many roles a short, realistic task (review a draft, triage five tickets, critique a dashboard) shows more than questions. Keep it short, the same for everyone, scored with anchors, and don't use candidates' work for real. Tell candidates how long it should take, and stick to it.

## Pitfalls

- Brainteasers or "tell me about yourself" as a scored question.
- Panelists all asking about the same competency while others go untested.
- Letting a candidate's confidence stand in for evidence.
- Writing anchors after the interviews to fit the candidate you liked.
