# Corrective feedback

Most learners make more errors in a session than they can absorb. The skill is choosing the few that matter and presenting them so they stick.

## Which errors to pick

Sort what you noticed into three bins, then correct from the top:

1. **Meaning breakers.** The listener would misunderstand ("I'm embarrassed" vs "I'm pregnant" in Spanish).
2. **Repeated patterns.** The same mistake three or more times (wrong past tense, missing article). One correction fixes many sentences.
3. **Reachable next steps.** Errors in structures they're just learning.

Leave the rest. Errors in structures well above their level are noise for now.

Aim for two or three items per digest. More than five, and none of them stick.

## Feedback types

| Type | What it looks like | When it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Recast | You repeat their sentence correctly in your reply, without comment | During conversation, keeps flow; easy to miss, so don't rely on it alone |
| Explicit correction | "We say *fui*, not *fue*, for 'I went'." | In the digest |
| Prompt | "Can you say that again in the past?" | When they know the rule but slipped; makes them do the work |
| Metalinguistic hint | "Check the verb ending." | Advanced learners who can self-correct |

Prompts make the learner produce the fix themselves, which tends to stick better than being told. Use them when you're confident they know the form.

## Digest template

```
## Session digest — <date>

What went well: <one specific thing, e.g. "You told the whole story in the past tense.">

Fix 1: <pattern name>
  You said: <their sentence>
  Better:   <corrected sentence>
  Why:      <one-line rule>
  Try:      <one sentence for them to produce using their own life>

Fix 2: …

New words to keep: <3–6 words, with their context sentences → deck>
```

## Worked example

Learner (B1 French) said three times: "Je suis allé à le marché."

```
Fix: à + le → au
  You said: Je suis allé à le marché.
  Better:   Je suis allé au marché.
  Why:      à + le always merges into "au" (and à + les into "aux").
  Try:      Tell me where you go on Sundays, using "au".
```

Log the pattern in the error file. If it appears again next week, it's a cluster worth a grammar mini-lesson.

## Common mistakes

- Correcting everything. It reads as a verdict, not help.
- Vague praise ("good job!"). Name what worked.
- Rules without examples. Always show the learner's sentence first.
- Correcting accent or pronunciation you can't reliably judge from text or audio. Flag it as "worth checking with a native speaker" instead.
- Ignoring emotional load. A learner who apologizes often needs more "what went well" and fewer fixes.
