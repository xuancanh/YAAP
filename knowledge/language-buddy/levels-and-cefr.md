# Levels and the CEFR

The CEFR (Common European Framework of Reference) is the most widely used scale. It describes what a learner *can do*, not how many words they know. Use it as shared vocabulary with the learner and to choose material.

## The six levels in everyday terms

| Level | Can do, roughly |
|---|---|
| A1 Beginner | Introduce themselves, ask and answer simple questions about familiar things, if the other person talks slowly and helps. |
| A2 Elementary | Handle routine exchanges: shopping, directions, describing family and job in simple terms. |
| B1 Intermediate | Get by while travelling, talk about experiences, plans and opinions with simple linking. |
| B2 Upper intermediate | Follow most of a conversation or show on familiar topics, argue a point, talk with natives without strain on either side. |
| C1 Advanced | Use the language flexibly at work or study; understand implicit meaning; few searches for words. |
| C2 Proficient | Understand virtually everything; express fine shades of meaning. |

The official descriptors are in the Council of Europe's global scale (linked in sources). Each level is wide: "B1" covers months or years of progress, so it helps to say "early B1" or "solid B1".

## Other scales you'll meet

- **JLPT (Japanese):** N5 (easiest) to N1. Tests reading and listening only, so a learner can pass N3 and still struggle to speak.
- **HSK (Mandarin):** the level structure was revised in recent years. Check the current official format before planning prep; don't assume older level counts.
- **ACTFL (US):** Novice, Intermediate, Advanced, Superior, each with sub-levels. Common in US schools.

Exam formats, scoring and dates change. Always confirm with the exam body before building an exam plan.

## Quick placement in one conversation

1. Start at A2 pace: short sentences, familiar topics (their day, their work).
2. Ask one question per level up: something about the past (A2), an opinion with a reason (B1), a hypothetical ("what would you do if…") (B2), an abstract topic (C1).
3. Note where they slow down, switch to their first language or answer in fragments. That's the edge. Place them one step below it for comfort and plan to stretch toward it.
4. Check reading separately if it matters. Reading level often runs ahead of speaking.

## Signs a learner is ready to move up

- They handle the current level's topics without switching language.
- Corrections are about polish, not meaning.
- They start reaching for structures from the next level on their own.
- Graded material feels easy; they're asking for "real" content.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the level as a score to chase rather than a description of use.
- Placing by vocabulary count. Two learners with the same word count can be a full level apart in speaking.
- Ignoring uneven profiles. Many learners are B2 listening and A2 speaking. Track skills separately in the profile.
