# Evaluating learning

The Kirkpatrick Model is a widely used framework for evaluating training. It has four levels: **Reaction** (did people find it engaging and relevant?), **Learning** (did they gain the intended knowledge, skills and confidence?), **Behaviour** (do they apply it on the job?) and **Results** (did the targeted outcomes happen?). Plan from Level 4 down: decide the result you want, then the behaviours that drive it, then what people need to learn.

Most of the value is at Levels 3 and 4. Most of the effort usually goes into Level 1. Rebalance.

## Plan the measures before you build

For each programme, fill in from the top:

| Level | Question | Measure | When | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 Results | Which key result should move? | e.g. content-assisted pipeline, review rounds per task | Next cycle | Metric tools |
| 3 Behaviour | What will people do differently at work? | e.g. briefs approved first time | 4–8 weeks after | Task records, manager check |
| 2 Learning | Can they do it in practice? | Before/after exercise scored on a rubric | Start and end | Programme |
| 1 Reaction | Was it relevant and usable? | Two or three questions | End | Short survey |

## Level 1: reaction

Keep it short and about usefulness, not enjoyment:
- How relevant was this to your work? (scale)
- What will you use first?
- What should we cut or change?

## Level 2: learning

Use a realistic exercise before and after, scored with the same rubric. Examples: rewrite a vague review comment; triage a set of tickets; write a brief from a scenario. A multiple-choice quiz checks knowledge, which is fine for compliance topics but weak for skills.

> **Learning gain** = average after-score − average before-score (on the same rubric)

## Level 3: behaviour (applied-skill signals)

This is where you prove the programme worked. Good signals are:

- **Observable in existing records**, so nobody has to fill in a form: task review rounds, acceptance on first review, recurring review comments, time to complete a standard task, escalation rate.
- **Directly linked** to the outcome you wrote.
- **Measured before and after** for the same group, and where possible compared with a group that hasn't been trained yet.

Examples:

| Programme | Applied-skill signal |
|---|---|
| Brief writing | Share of briefs approved without a second round |
| Giving feedback (managers) | Share of review comments that are specific (sampled and rated against a checklist) |
| Data reporting | Share of weekly reports delivered without analyst help |
| Escalation handling | Escalations resolved at first level |

Also ask managers a single question 4–8 weeks later: "Have you seen {behaviour} in their work? Example?" Count examples, not opinions.

## Level 4: results

Show the trend in the key result alongside the behaviour signal, and be honest about attribution: many things move a key result. Say "briefs approved first time rose from X to Y over the quarter, and content cycle time fell; the training is likely one contributor" rather than claiming the programme caused the whole change.

## What to do with the findings

- Behaviour didn't change but learning did: the problem is transfer (manager support, time, tools). Fix follow-through, not content.
- Learning didn't change: the design or practice is weak. Fix the programme.
- Reaction is poor on relevance: the audience or the gap was wrong.
- Results didn't move but behaviour did: the capability may not be the main driver of the key result. Revisit the gap analysis.

## Pitfalls

- Reporting satisfaction scores as success.
- Choosing a behaviour signal after the programme, to fit the data.
- Measuring individuals in shared files. Report by group; individual results stay with the person and their manager.
- Claiming the whole movement in a key result.
