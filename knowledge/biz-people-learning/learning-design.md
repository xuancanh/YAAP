# Designing learning that transfers

The test of a programme is whether people do the work differently a month later. Design for that from the start: outcome first, practice in the middle, follow-through at the end.

## Write the outcome

One to three outcomes, each describing something a person will do at work that someone else could observe.

Formula: **[Who] will [do what], [to what standard], [in what situation].**

- Weak: "Understand how to write good briefs."
- Strong: "Content marketers will write a brief that the Head of Marketing approves without a second round, for any new long-form piece."

If you can't write the outcome this way, the gap isn't clear enough yet. Go back to the gap analysis.

## Choose the format

| Need | Format |
|---|---|
| People need a reference at the moment of work | A guide, checklist or playbook (often the whole answer) |
| A skill that needs reps | Practice exercises with feedback, done on real or realistic work |
| A shared method for a team | A short workshop plus practice and a reference |
| Judgment that's learned by example | Coaching, mentoring, pairing, reviewing examples together |
| Knowledge that must be checked (e.g. compliance) | Short module plus a check of understanding |

Prefer the lightest format that will work. A one-page checklist that people use beats a half-day course they forget.

## Structure each session or module

1. **Why it matters** (a few minutes): the key result it affects and a real example of the problem.
2. **The method**: as few steps as possible, with one good and one poor example.
3. **Practice**: most of the time. A realistic task, done individually or in pairs.
4. **Feedback**: against a short checklist or rubric, from a facilitator or peer.
5. **Plan to apply**: each participant names the next real piece of work where they'll use it.

## Make practice count

- **Realistic.** Use the company's own work (anonymised if needed): a real draft, a real dataset, a real situation.
- **Specific feedback.** Tied to the checklist, about the work, with what to change.
- **Repeated.** A second attempt after feedback is where most of the learning happens.
- **Spaced.** Short practice spread over a few weeks tends to stick better than one long session. Plan a follow-up exercise rather than a longer first session.
- **Recall, not re-reading.** Ask people to apply or explain the method from memory rather than rereading slides.

## Follow-through at work

Learning that isn't used fades. Before launch, agree with managers:

- the first real task where each person will apply it, and when;
- that the manager will look at that work and give feedback against the same checklist;
- a short check-in a few weeks later: what worked, what got in the way.

Managers are the biggest factor in whether learning is applied. Brief them before the programme, not after.

## Reuse before you build

Check `org_playbooks` and the knowledge base first. If a good playbook exists, the programme may just be "practise the playbook with feedback". If you write a new guide, offer it as a playbook so it outlives the programme.

## Plan template

```
Gap:           (from the gap analysis, with the key result)
Outcome(s):    Who will do what, to what standard, in what situation
Audience:      Teams and roles; count
Format:        and why it's the lightest that will work
Outline:       Sessions or materials, timings, practice tasks
Follow-through: First real task per person; manager feedback; check-in date
Measures:      Completion; before/after check; work signal (see evaluation doc)
Owner, dates:  Build, launch, check-in, review
```

See [evaluating learning](evaluating-learning.md) for choosing the measures.

## Pitfalls

- Content-first design ("let's cover everything about SEO").
- Slides with no practice.
- No manager involvement, then surprise that nothing changed.
- Long programmes for a narrow gap.
