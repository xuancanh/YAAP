# Discussion question craft

A good club question has no right answer, can be argued from the text, and makes people disagree. Publisher reading-guide questions often fail on all three.

## What makes a question work

- **Open, not factual.** "Why does Stevens never say what he feels?" not "What job does Stevens have?"
- **Grounded.** People can point to a page.
- **Divisive.** Reasonable readers will disagree. If everyone would say yes, cut it.
- **Short.** One sentence. Long questions get answered with "Yes, interesting".
- **Personal, then textual.** A personal question warms up the room; textual ones keep it about the book.

## The question ladder

Plan roughly two of each, then pick on the night.

1. **Reaction.** "Which character would you least want to be stuck with on a train, and why?"
2. **Craft.** "Why tell this story out of order? What would we lose if it were chronological?"
3. **Character choice.** "Was she right to leave? Argue the other side."
4. **Theme.** "Is this a book about grief or about guilt?"
5. **Ending.** "Did the ending earn itself, or did it cheat?"
6. **World.** "Would this story work set today?"

Have one "if it stalls" question in reserve that almost always gets people going, such as "Who was the real villain?" or "What would you cut?"

## Session plan template

```
Book:            Host:          Date:
Length: ~90 min

0–10   Arrival, food, quick round: one word for the book
10–20  First reactions: did you finish? Love/hate on a 1–5 show of hands
20–60  Main questions (3–4 from the ladder, in order of energy)
60–75  Ending and big-picture question
75–85  Rating round; "who should read this?"
85–90  Next book pick and date
Notes: passages to read aloud (page refs), any context worth sharing
```

## Handling the room

- **Those who didn't finish.** Decide up front: spoilers allowed. Say so in the invitation.
- **One voice dominating.** Go round the table for a single question.
- **Quiet members.** Give them a heads-up of one question in advance.
- **Off-topic drift.** Bring it back with a passage: "Let's read the bit on page 112."
- **Everyone hated it.** Great. Ask what the book was trying to do and why it failed. Some of the best sessions come from this.

## Context that helps

A two-minute brief on the author or setting can add depth: when it was written, what was happening, how it was first received. Keep it short and accurate, and check facts rather than relying on memory.

## Worked example: *The Remains of the Day*

- Reaction: "Did you like Stevens by the end? Did that change?"
- Craft: "Stevens is an unreliable narrator. Where did you first stop believing him?"
- Choice: "Should Miss Kenton have stayed? Should he have asked her?"
- Theme: "Is dignity in this book a virtue or a trap?"
- Ending: "Is the pier scene hopeful or devastating?"
- World: "Who are the Stevenses of today's workplaces?"
- Stall-breaker: "What's the most British sentence in the book?"

## Common mistakes

- Twelve questions for a ninety-minute session; four good ones is plenty.
- Questions that test whether people read it.
- Opening with the ending.
