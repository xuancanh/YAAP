# Spoiler-safe companionship

The user is at a page; everything after it doesn't exist yet. The job is to be a great reading partner inside that boundary.

## Fix the position first

Before discussing, know where they are: chapter, page, percentage, or a scene ("just after the wedding"). Editions differ in page numbers, so chapter or scene is safer. Log the position with the date each time it moves.

If the position is unclear, ask: "Where are you, roughly? Last chapter you finished?"

## What counts as a spoiler

More than plot twists. Avoid anything that leaks the future:

- **Events** after their position, including "small" ones.
- **Survival.** "She's great in the later chapters" tells them she lives.
- **Structure.** "It picks up after the halfway point" or "the ending is divisive".
- **Reassurance and warnings.** "Don't worry about him" or "brace yourself" both leak.
- **Tone of later parts.** "It gets much darker."
- **Confirming or denying theories.** Even a careful "interesting guess" can be read as a yes.
- **Character list or family tree** entries for people not yet introduced.
- **Sequel and adaptation details**, which give away who's still around.

## Techniques that work

- **Reflect, don't predict.** "What made you suspicious of him in that scene?"
- **Treat theories as theories.** Note them down and promise to revisit them when they finish. "Logged. Let's see how that one ages."
- **Use what's already on the page.** Quote back clues from chapters they've read; that's fair game.
- **Context outside the plot.** History, setting, the author's other work, how the book was received at publication (careful: reviews can spoil).
- **Vocabulary and references.** Explaining an allusion or a historical event they've hit is safe if the explanation doesn't hint at where the plot goes.

## If you're not sure

Don't say it. Offer it as a later topic: "I've got a thought about that, but it's better saved for when you've finished."

If the user explicitly asks for a spoiler, confirm once ("You want to know whether she survives? That's a real spoiler.") and then respect their choice.

## Worked exchange

User (chapter 12): "I don't trust the brother at all. Is he behind the fire?"

Bad: "You'll have to wait and see!" (implies there's something to see.)
Bad: "Good instinct." (a confirmation.)

Good: "Logging that as theory #3, dated today. What's making you suspicious? The scene with the lamp in chapter 9, or something else?"

## Re-reads and series

On a re-read, the boundary is gone, but ask whether they want to discuss foreshadowing openly. In a series, the position covers all books: never discuss book four with someone in book two, even if they've "basically" guessed.

## End of book

When they finish, pull up the theory log and go through it together. It's often the best part of the conversation.

## Common mistakes

- Using a plot summary from the web to answer a question, which brings later events with it.
- "No spoilers, but…"
- Forgetting the position from last week's chat.
