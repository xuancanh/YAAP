# Fairness, confidentiality and legal lines

Employment law varies by country, state and sometimes city, and it changes. This doc isn't legal advice. It tells you where the lines usually are so you can stop, keep the record clean, and hand the question to a qualified HR or employment-law professional before anyone acts.

## Protected characteristics (examples, not a complete list)

Most jurisdictions forbid treating people worse because of certain characteristics. Two well-known lists:

- **United States (federal, enforced by the EEOC):** race, colour, religion, sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation and transgender status), national origin, age (40 or older), disability and genetic information (including family medical history). Retaliation for complaining about discrimination is also prohibited. State and local laws often add more.
- **United Kingdom (Equality Act 2010):** age, gender reassignment, marriage and civil partnership, pregnancy and maternity, disability, race (including colour, nationality and ethnic or national origin), religion or belief, sex, sexual orientation.

Check the company's jurisdictions. If `/org/people/policies/` doesn't say which laws apply, that's a question for a human, not something to assume.

## What that means in practice

- Don't ask about, record, or score candidates or employees on protected characteristics. That includes questions that act as proxies: graduation year as an age proxy, "do you have kids?", "where are you originally from?".
- Diversity and equal-opportunity data, where it's collected lawfully, lives in the HR system, separate from hiring decisions, and is reported only in aggregate above the anonymity threshold.
- Job requirements must be job-related. "Native speaker", "recent graduate" or "digital native" can be discriminatory. Use the actual skill ("professional written English").
- Treat health information, accommodation requests, leave, and anything about a complaint as special and highly confidential.

## Always a human decision, with qualified advice

Stop and route these to the accountable human, recommending HR or legal advice:

- Dismissal, redundancy, ending a probation, or any disciplinary outcome.
- A complaint or grievance, especially harassment, discrimination or whistleblowing. Acknowledge it, protect confidentiality, don't investigate on your own, and escalate the same day.
- Accommodation or adjustment requests related to disability, pregnancy or religion.
- Pay decisions, pay-equity questions, and anything about a named person's compensation.
- Immigration and right-to-work questions.
- Background checks, references and monitoring of employees.
- Anything with a legal deadline (claims, tribunal or court letters, regulator contact).

For each, write a short case on the task (facts, dates, what policy says, the open question) and raise it with org_escalate. Drafts to employees or candidates are drafts for a human to approve and send.

## Personal data

- Individual records (scorecards, reviews, health, pay, complaints) live only in the HR system the company grants. They don't belong in `/org/`, agent home folders, channels or meeting notes, because those are readable by every member.
- Shared reports use counts and roles, never names alongside performance, pay, health or exit reasons.
- Collect only what the purpose needs, keep it only as long as policy says, and give access only to those who need it.
- Requests from employees for their own data are legally time-bound in many places. Route them to the legal unit the day they arrive.

## Bias checks you can run in aggregate

- Pass-through rates by stage for groups above the anonymity threshold, where the data is held lawfully.
- Whether scorecards were completed for every interview, and before the debrief.
- Rating distributions by manager in calibration.
- Whether exit reasons cluster in one team.

Report patterns to the accountable humans with the evidence. Don't speculate about individuals.

## Common pitfalls

- **"Just checking" questions** in interviews or 1:1s that touch protected areas.
- **Investigating a complaint yourself.** It can compromise the process. Route it.
- **Putting a named person's situation in a weekly report** because it explains a number. Say "one regretted exit in Support" and nothing more.
- **Assuming one country's rules apply everywhere** the company employs people.
- **Writing advice as if it were settled law.** Say what the policy says, what you don't know, and who should decide.
