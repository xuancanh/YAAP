# Headline and structure checklist

Run this on the outline, then again on the finished draft. Most review comments on content are structural, not grammatical: the point is buried, sections wander, or the headline promises something the piece doesn't deliver.

## Headlines

A good headline tells the reader exactly what they get and who it's for. Try at least five, then pick.

- [ ] **Specific promise.** "How to cut month-end close from 10 days to 5" beats "Improving your close".
- [ ] **Matches the content.** If the piece delivers a checklist, say checklist. Clickbait raises clicks and lowers trust and engagement.
- [ ] **Reader's words, not ours.** Use the term the reader searches or says, not our internal feature name.
- [ ] **Front-loaded.** The important words come first; long headlines get cut off in search results and inboxes.
- [ ] **No unsupported superlatives.** "The best", "the only", "#1" need proof or come out.

Useful patterns (pick the one that fits the job):

- How to + outcome: "How to write a brief your editor approves first time"
- Number + specific noun: "7 reconciliations every close needs"
- Question the reader asks: "What should a month-end checklist include?"
- Outcome + constraint: "A content calendar for a team of one"

## Intro (first 100 or so words)

- [ ] States the reader's problem in one or two sentences.
- [ ] Says what the piece gives them and, for guides, the answer up front.
- [ ] No throat-clearing ("In today's fast-paced world…"), no history lesson.
- [ ] If it's for search, the main query's meaning appears naturally early.

## Structure

- [ ] **Inverted pyramid in each section:** the point first, then explanation, then example.
- [ ] **H2s read as a summary.** Someone who only reads the headings should get the gist.
- [ ] **One idea per paragraph,** short paragraphs, sentences mostly under about 25 words.
- [ ] **Lists for steps and options,** tables for comparisons, prose for reasoning.
- [ ] **Examples are concrete:** a real number from a source, a named (approved) customer, a screenshot.
- [ ] **One primary CTA,** placed where the reader has just got value, repeated at the end.
- [ ] Ends with a next step, not a summary of what they just read.

## Claims and sources

- [ ] Every statistic, quote and factual claim has an inline source a reviewer can open.
- [ ] Sources are primary where possible (the study, not a blog quoting it) and recent enough for the claim.
- [ ] Anything marked unverified is resolved or removed before review.
- [ ] Customer names, logos and quotes have approval on file.
- [ ] Comparisons with competitors are accurate today and fair. Check with the Product Marketer's competitor brief.

## Style guide pass

- [ ] Voice and tone follow `/org/marketing/style-guide.md`.
- [ ] Terms match the glossary (product names, capitalisation, words we avoid).
- [ ] Inclusive language; no idioms that won't translate if the audience is international.
- [ ] Links have descriptive text ("see the close checklist"), never "click here".
- [ ] Images have alt text that says what the image shows and why it's there.

## Final self-edit (ten minutes, before `org_request_review`)

1. Re-read the brief. Does the draft serve that reader, that job, that angle? Note how in the review request.
2. Read only the headline, intro and H2s. Is the story clear?
3. Cut 10 percent: adverbs, repeated points, hedges like "very", "really", "in order to".
4. Read the CTA as the reader. Is it obvious what happens when they click?
5. Check the meta title, meta description and suggested internal links are present.

## Common failures

- **The buried lead:** the useful answer is in paragraph six. Move it up.
- **The listicle with no thread:** ten tips with no order or priority. Group them or rank them.
- **The feature tour:** the product appears before the reader's problem is solved. Solve first, mention second.
- **The word-count chase:** padding to hit a length. Length should follow what the reader needs.
