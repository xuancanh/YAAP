# Logic model and outcomes

A logic model is one page that says: we put in these resources, do these things, which produce these results, which lead to these changes. Funders ask for it in many forms (theory of change, results framework). The impact file is organised around it.

## The five boxes

| Box | Question | Example: food bank |
|---|---|---|
| **Inputs** | What do we use? | Volunteers, warehouse, donated food, a van, funding |
| **Activities** | What do we do? | Collect, sort and distribute food parcels; benefits advice sessions |
| **Outputs** | What do we produce, counted? | Parcels given out, households served, advice sessions held |
| **Outcomes** | What changes for people? | Households report fewer days without enough food; families claim benefits they were entitled to |
| **Impact** | What longer-term change are we contributing to? | Reduced food insecurity in the area |

Outputs are *how much you did*. Outcomes are *what difference it made*. Funders increasingly want outcomes; most small orgs only count outputs. The aim is to add one or two outcome measures you can realistically collect.

## Writing outcome statements

A good outcome statement says who, what changes, by how much and by when.

- Weak: "Improve wellbeing for young people."
- Better: "By the end of the 10-week program, at least two-thirds of participants report feeling more confident speaking in a group, measured with a before-and-after question."

Check each statement:

- [ ] Who exactly?
- [ ] What changes (knowledge, behaviour, situation, feeling)?
- [ ] How will you know? (a question, an observation, a record)
- [ ] When?
- [ ] Is the target realistic and in your control to influence?

Set targets from your own past data where you have it. Don't copy figures from elsewhere.

## Light-touch evidence methods

| Method | Effort | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Counts at the point of service | Low | Outputs |
| One or two before-and-after questions | Low | Change in confidence, knowledge |
| Short follow-up call or text a month later | Medium | Whether the change lasted |
| Case stories (with consent) | Low | Showing how change happens |
| Partner data (school attendance, referrals) | Varies | Situational change; needs agreements and data protection care |

Keep surveys short. Two good questions answered by most people beat twenty answered by a few.

## Attribution: be honest

Many things affect people's lives. Say "contributed to" rather than "caused", and report what you measured, not what you hoped. Funders trust honest variance notes more than perfect numbers.

## Worked example: mentoring program

- Inputs: 12 volunteer mentors, coordinator 2 days a week
- Activities: weekly one-hour mentoring sessions for 6 months
- Outputs: 20 young people matched; sessions held; attendance rate
- Outcome 1: by month 6, most mentees report a clear next step (job, course, training), from an exit question
- Outcome 2: at a 3-month follow-up, number of mentees in education, training or work, from a follow-up text
- Story: two consented case studies a year

## Common mistakes

- Calling outputs outcomes ("we delivered 500 sessions" is not a change).
- Too many outcomes. Pick the two or three that matter most.
- Collecting data nobody uses. Every measure should feed a report or a decision.
- Reconstructing numbers in December. Log as it happens.
