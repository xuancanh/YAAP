# Recommendation letter kit

A strong letter is specific: what the writer saw the student do, compared with peers, and why it predicts success in the next role. Most of that comes from a good intake.

## Intake form (send when a student asks)

- Name, how you know them (course codes, terms, research role), grades they're happy for you to mention
- What they're applying for, with links; the deadline and how letters are submitted
- Their CV and draft personal statement
- Three things they'd like you to be able to speak to, with specific examples (a project, a moment in class)
- Whether they've waived their right to see the letter, if the application asks (US applications often do)
- Written consent to share their education information in the letter, if your institution requires it

Ask for at least three or four weeks' notice. A request without intake materials goes back with the form.

## Letter structure

1. **Opening:** who you are, your relationship to the student, how long, and in what capacity. One line of overall endorsement.
2. **Evidence paragraphs (two or three):** each built on a specific example tied to what the programme or employer values. Concrete actions and results.
3. **Comparison:** where they stand among students you've taught (only if true and you can support it).
4. **Fit:** why these qualities matter for this opportunity.
5. **Close:** an unambiguous recommendation and an offer to be contacted.

Keep it to about a page unless the field expects more.

## Wording checks

Research on reference letters has found that letters for women and men can differ in patterns of language. Check each draft:

- Are achievements described as strongly as character? ("Brilliant analyst", not only "hard-working and caring")
- Any doubt-raisers: "she may not be the strongest, but…", hedges, faint praise?
- Personal details irrelevant to the role (appearance, family, health)?
- Are adjectives backed by evidence?
- Same length and specificity as letters you write for similar students?

## Privacy and consent

In the US, FERPA protects education records; grades and other record information generally need the student's written consent before they're disclosed. Other countries have their own rules. Follow the institution's policy and ask the registrar if unsure. Mention accommodations or health only if the student explicitly asks and it serves them.

## Tracking

Per request: student initials, programme, deadline, submission method, status (intake / draft / professor review / submitted / confirmed). Draft at least a week before the deadline. After submission, save the final version for future requests from the same student, and tell them it's in.

## When to decline

If the professor can't write a strong, specific letter, say so early and kindly; a lukewarm letter can hurt more than none.

> Thanks for asking. I don't think I know your work well enough to write the strong letter you deserve. Dr. X, who supervised your project, may be better placed.

## Common mistakes

- Generic letters reused with a name swapped (wrong programme name left in).
- Restating the CV instead of adding observations.
- Missing the portal deadline because the invitation email went to spam.
