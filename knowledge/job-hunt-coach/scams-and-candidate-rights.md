# Scams and candidate rights

Job seekers are a target for scams, and interviews sometimes stray into questions they shouldn't. Both are worth checking for on every application.

## Job scam red flags

The US Federal Trade Commission warns about patterns like these:

- an unexpected job offer by text, call, email or messaging app, from someone you didn't contact;
- quick, easy money for little effort;
- a request to pay for training, equipment, certification or "placement";
- a request for bank details, ID documents or your national ID number before any real interview;
- a cheque sent to you, with instructions to deposit it and send part of the money on;
- jobs that only involve receiving and reshipping packages;
- recruiters using personal email addresses, or interviews done only by chat;
- impersonation of real, well-known companies.

## What to do

1. **Verify independently.** Find the company's real website or phone number yourself, and check the job is listed there.
2. **Never pay to get a job,** and don't share sensitive details until you've confirmed the employer and have a written offer.
3. **Don't forward money or packages** for an employer.
4. **Report it.** In the US, at ReportFraud.ftc.gov; elsewhere, the national fraud reporting service, plus the job board where it was posted.
5. **If money or details were already sent:** contact the bank straight away, and follow the national guidance on identity theft.

## Interview questions that may be off-limits

This is general information; laws differ by country and, in the US, by state and city.

In the US, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission enforces federal laws that ban employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex (its guidance includes pregnancy, sexual orientation and transgender status), national origin, age (40 or older), disability and genetic information. Interpretation in this area has been shifting, so check the EEOC's current pages. Asking about these isn't always illegal by itself, but it can be evidence of discrimination if used to decide.

Disability is stricter: under EEOC guidance, before a job offer an employer generally may not ask disability-related questions or require medical exams. They may ask whether you can do the job's tasks, with or without reasonable accommodation.

Other countries have their own equality laws; for example, the UK's Equality Act 2010 covers similar characteristics.

## Handling an inappropriate question in the moment

Options, from soft to firm:

- **Answer the concern behind it:** asked about children, "I'm fully able to meet the hours and travel this role needs."
- **Redirect:** "I'd rather focus on my experience with…"
- **Name it calmly:** "I don't think that's relevant to the role. Could you tell me more about the team?"

Afterwards, write down the question, who asked it, and when.

## When to get advice

If the user thinks they were turned down because of a protected characteristic, suggest they:

- keep notes, emails and the job ad;
- contact the national equality body or labour agency (in the US, the EEOC; deadlines for complaints apply);
- talk to an employment lawyer or, in the UK, advice services such as Acas or Citizens Advice.

Don't tell the user whether they have a legal case; that's for a lawyer.

## Worked example

A "recruiter" on a messaging app offers a remote data-entry job paying well, with an interview by chat and a request for bank details to "set up payroll." The agent flags four red flags (unsolicited, chat-only interview, early bank request, too-good pay), suggests checking the company's official careers page, finds no such role, and drafts a short report to the job board and ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
