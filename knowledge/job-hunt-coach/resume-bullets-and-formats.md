# Resume bullets and formats

Recruiters skim. Each bullet should say what changed because the person was there.

## The bullet formula

```
[Action verb] + [what you did] + [result, with a number or concrete outcome] + (optional: how / for whom)
```

| Duty (weak) | Achievement (strong) |
|---|---|
| Responsible for customer emails | Cut average reply time from 2 days to 4 hours by setting up shared templates and a triage rota |
| Managed social media | Grew the newsletter from 1,200 to 5,000 subscribers in a year by switching to weekly how-to posts |
| Worked on the billing system | Rebuilt invoice generation, removing a manual step that took finance a day each month |

The numbers in these examples are illustrations. Use the user's real figures; if they don't know them, use a concrete outcome instead ("adopted by all three regional teams").

## Questions that draw out results

Ask role by role:

1. What was broken or slow when you arrived?
2. What did you change?
3. What happened as a result? Anything measurable: time, money, volume, errors, customers?
4. What are you proudest of from that job?
5. What did your manager thank you for?
6. What would have gone wrong without you?

## Mirroring the posting, honestly

- Use the posting's words for skills the user really has ("stakeholder management" if that's their term for it).
- Put the most relevant evidence first in each role.
- Don't claim a skill just because it's listed. Interviews expose it.
- Many employers use applicant tracking systems to sort applications. Plain formatting (no text in images, standard headings, a simple layout) keeps the text readable to them and to people.

## Gaps, short stints and career changes

- **Gaps:** a short, true line works better than hiding it. "Career break: caring for a family member (2024-2025)" or "Sabbatical: travel and a data analysis course."
- **Short stints:** be brief and factual ("contract role", "company restructured"). Prepare a one-sentence explanation for the interview.
- **Career change:** lead with a short profile that links past experience to the new field, and list transferable achievements and recent training.
- **Older candidates:** it's common to show the last 10-15 years in detail and summarise earlier roles.

## Country conventions

These are common norms; check for the specific country and industry.

| | US resume | UK CV | Many European countries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Length | often 1 page early career, 2 later | usually 2 pages | 1-2 pages; Europass format in some sectors |
| Photo | no | no | sometimes expected (e.g. in parts of Germany or France); check |
| Date of birth, marital status | no | no | sometimes included; increasingly optional |
| Personal statement | short summary | short profile common | varies |

In the US and UK, leaving out photos, age and marital status is standard and helps avoid bias.

## Cover letter shape

Three short paragraphs: why this company (something specific and checkable), the two strongest matches between the role and the user's evidence, and the close. No "I am writing to express my interest".

## Common mistakes

- Duties instead of results.
- One generic resume for every application.
- Unexplained acronyms from a previous employer.
- Inflated titles or numbers. Background checks and reference calls catch them.
- Typos in the name of the company you're applying to.

## Worked example

User's line: "Ran the warehouse night shift." Questions reveal they reorganised pick routes and cut mis-shipments. They don't know the exact figure, but the client complaints log went from weekly to rare. Rewritten: "Led a 12-person night shift; redesigned pick routes and labelling, which cut mis-shipment complaints from weekly to rare within six months." The user confirmed each fact.
