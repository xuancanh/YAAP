# Non-standard terms primer

Buyers often ask for terms outside the company's standard. Some only cost money; others create legal or financial risk that lasts for years. Know what each one does so you can route it correctly and explain the trade-off.

> This is a general primer, not legal or accounting advice. Legal owns contract terms and finance owns revenue and payment rules. How a term is treated can depend on jurisdiction and accounting standards; route to them.

## Commercial terms (usually finance and the pricing policy)

| Term | What it is | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Extended payment terms** | paying later than standard (e.g. longer than the standard net days) | delays cash; increases collection risk |
| **Monthly or quarterly billing** on an annual contract | smaller, more frequent invoices | delays cash; more admin; churn risk if the customer can stop paying |
| **Price lock / renewal cap** | renewal price fixed or uplift capped | limits future revenue; must be tracked to renewal |
| **Ramp pricing** | price increases over the term | lowers early revenue; the effective discount must be calculated over the term |
| **Free months or extended trial** | service before or beyond the paid term | real discount; may affect revenue timing |
| **Most favoured customer (MFN)** | promise that no similar customer gets a better price | very hard to monitor and limits future deals; usually needs senior approval or is refused |
| **Volume commitments with true-up** | customer commits to a quantity and pays for overages later | needs a clear true-up mechanism and billing process |

## Legal and risk terms (route to legal)

| Term | What it is | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Termination for convenience** | customer can end the contract early without cause | the committed term may not be real; affects bookings and forecast |
| **Changes to limitation of liability** | raising or removing caps on damages | increases financial exposure |
| **Uncapped or broader indemnities** | we cover more of the customer's losses | potentially large exposure |
| **Service level credits** beyond standard | money back for missed uptime or support targets | cost and operational commitments |
| **Data location or processing terms** | where and how data is stored or processed | must match what the product and infrastructure support |
| **Acceptance clauses** | payment or contract depends on the customer accepting delivery | can delay or put revenue at risk |
| **Auto-renewal removed** | contract ends unless both sides re-sign | renewal risk; CS must start renewal earlier |
| **Assignment and change of control** | rules if either party is acquired | matters for both sides' future flexibility |

## Product and delivery commitments (route to product or services)

- **Feature commitments** ("will deliver X by date") — avoid in contracts; if unavoidable, product must approve in writing.
- **Custom development** — needs a scope, price and owner in services.
- **Dedicated resources** (named CSM, support hours) — must match what the customer team can deliver.

## How to handle a request

1. Ask the AE for the buyer's underlying concern. A request to remove auto-renewal might really be a need for budget approval each year, which another term can solve.
2. Check whether the policy or legal's fallback positions already cover it (many legal teams have pre-approved alternative wording).
3. If not, prepare an exception (see [exception case template](exception-case-template.md)) with the term, the buyer's reason, the risk and the alternatives.
4. Record the approved term in the deal record so finance, CS and the renewal team see it.

## Watch for terms hiding in free text

Comments fields, email threads and "notes to customer" can create commitments. Flag anything that sounds like a promise about price, features, cancellation or service levels, and route it as a term.
