# Legal requirements checklist

The ceremony is what guests see. The legal steps are what make the marriage valid, and they come with fixed deadlines. **The rules depend on the country and often on the state, county or registration district, and they change.** Treat this as a list of questions to research and confirm, not as answers. The registrar, county clerk or officiant has the final word.

## Questions to research for any jurisdiction

- [ ] **Who registers marriages here?** The register office, county clerk, civil registry or a religious body with legal authority.
- [ ] **Notice or licence:** do you give notice in advance, or get a licence? How far ahead, and how long is it valid?
- [ ] **Residency:** do either of you need to have lived in the area for a set period before giving notice?
- [ ] **Documents:** ID, proof of address, birth certificates, proof that earlier marriages have ended (divorce decree or death certificate), and translations or apostilles for foreign documents.
- [ ] **Nationality and immigration:** extra steps if either partner isn't a national. **Refer to an immigration adviser** for anything visa-related.
- [ ] **Officiant:** who can legally conduct the ceremony? Is a religious ceremony legally binding on its own, or do you also need a civil one?
- [ ] **Venue:** must it be licensed or approved for marriages?
- [ ] **Witnesses:** how many, and are there age or ID rules?
- [ ] **Wording:** are any words legally required in the ceremony?
- [ ] **After the ceremony:** who files the paperwork, and how do you get certified copies of the certificate?

Record each answer in decisions.md with its source URL and the date you checked.

## Worked example 1: England and Wales (checked 2026-11-04)

According to GOV.UK:

- You must **give notice at least 29 days before the ceremony**, usually by appointment at your local register office. You must have lived in that registration district for the past 7 days.
- The ceremony must take place **within 12 months** of giving notice.
- Partners in different districts give notice separately. Extra rules apply if a partner is from outside the UK or Ireland.
- At a civil ceremony, a registrar must carry out or be present at the ceremony, and you need **at least 2 witnesses**.
- Anglican weddings may follow a different notice process. Check with the venue.

Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own systems. Don't apply these rules there.

## Worked example 2: United States

There's no single national process. Marriage licences are issued locally, under state law, usually by a county clerk or similar office. USA.gov notes that most licences expire somewhere between 30 days and a year, depending on the state. Waiting periods, fees, ID rules and officiant rules all vary. Check the county office where the ceremony will take place, not where the couple lives.

## Destination or overseas weddings

- The marriage must meet the **host country's** legal requirements. These can include residency periods, documents issued only a few months earlier, apostilled and translated papers, and in-person appointments.
- Some couples **marry legally at home** and hold a symbolic ceremony abroad. This is often simpler. Raise it as an option.
- Check how the marriage will be **recognised** in the couple's home country, using official government guidance.
- Build the document timeline backwards from the host country's deadlines, and allow time for apostilles and translations.

## When to refer on

- A previous marriage abroad, or a divorce that isn't finalised: a family lawyer.
- Immigration or visa questions: an immigration adviser.
- Prenuptial agreements: a lawyer for each partner.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming a beautiful ceremony equals a legal marriage. Check the officiant's authority.
- Missing the notice window because the venue was booked first.
- Bringing photocopies when originals or certified copies are required.
- Following another country's rules from a friend's wedding.
