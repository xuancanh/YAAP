# Sleep basics

This is general education, not treatment. Sleep needs vary between people, and persistent sleep problems are a medical issue for a doctor.

## How much sleep (population guidelines)

The CDC's guideline for adults is **7 or more hours a night for ages 18–60**, **7–9 hours for 61–64**, and **7–8 hours for 65 and older** (CDC, May 2024). Treat these as a reference, not a target to stress over. How someone feels during the day matters as much as the number.

## The well-established habits

Both the CDC and NHS recommend:

- **Keep a consistent schedule.** Go to bed and get up at about the same times every day. The NHS adds: keep your usual wake time even after a poor night. Wake time is usually the best lever to start with.
- **Make the bedroom quiet, dark, cool and comfortable.**
- **Wind down.** The NHS suggests relaxing for an hour before bed. The CDC suggests turning off devices at least 30 minutes before bed.
- **Watch caffeine, alcohol and big meals.** The CDC says to skip caffeine in the afternoon and evening, and to avoid large meals and alcohol before bed. The NHS suggests avoiding tea, coffee, alcohol and smoking for 6 hours before bed.
- **Stay active in the day.** The NHS adds: not within 4 hours of bedtime.
- **Avoid daytime naps** if night sleep is a problem (NHS guidance for insomnia).
- **Don't drive when drowsy.**

Change one or two things at a time, so you can tell what helped.

## Wind-down routine worksheet

Build it with the person, from things they would actually do:

```
Target lights-out: <time>     Wind-down starts: <time − 45–60 min>
1. <screen-off cue, e.g. phone on charger in the hall>
2. <calming activity: shower / reading / stretching / music>
3. <park the thoughts: write tomorrow's list or worries on paper>
4. <environment: lights low, room cool>
Bad-night rule: <e.g. if awake and frustrated, get up, do something calm in dim light, return when sleepy>
```

The bad-night rule matches the NHS tip to go to bed only when sleepy. Lying awake frustrated tends to make things worse.

## Light sleep log (one week, sleep.md)

| Date | In bed | Asleep (est.) | Woke | Out of bed | Caffeine after 2pm? | Screens last hour? | Felt (1 word) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Keep it light. A week of honest notes beats guessing, but tracking every night forever can make some people more anxious about sleep. Review it weekly in the check-in: has the trend improved, and which single lever comes next?

## When to see a doctor

The NHS advises seeing a GP if:

- changing sleep habits hasn't helped;
- trouble sleeping has gone on for months;
- insomnia is making it hard to cope with daily life.

Also suggest a doctor for:

- loud snoring with pauses in breathing or gasping (often noticed by a partner);
- feeling exhausted despite getting enough hours;
- falling asleep unintentionally during the day, especially while driving;
- unusual movements or behaviours during sleep;
- sleep problems that started with a new medication. They should talk to the prescriber, and not stop or change anything themselves.

Never suggest sleep medicines, supplements or doses. That's for a doctor or pharmacist.

## Worked example

Sam says they're "always tired". The week's log shows bedtimes between 23:00 and 01:30, a wake time of 07:00 on weekdays and 10:30 at weekends, and coffee at 4pm most days.

- **Lever 1:** a fixed 07:30 wake time every day, including weekends.
- **Lever 2** (from week 2): no coffee after 1pm.
- **Wind-down:** phone charging outside the bedroom from 23:00.

Review after two weeks. If Sam is still exhausted despite sleeping more regularly, suggest a GP visit.

## Common mistakes

- Changing five things at once.
- Turning sleep into a performance score.
- Lying in at weekends to "catch up". It shifts the body clock.
- Missing the signs that need a doctor.
