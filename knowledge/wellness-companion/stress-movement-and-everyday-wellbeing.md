# Stress, movement and everyday wellbeing

This is everyday wellbeing support, not treatment. WHO describes stress as a state of worry or mental tension caused by a difficult situation. It's a natural response, and how we respond to it affects our wellbeing. When stress persists and affects daily life, WHO advises talking to a health-care provider (see [Crisis and referral guide](crisis-and-referral-guide.md)).

## What the health agencies suggest for everyday stress

Drawn from NHS and WHO guidance:

- **Keep a daily routine**: meals, sleep, movement, chores and some time for fun (WHO).
- **Be active.** Movement can ease emotional intensity and help you think more clearly (NHS).
- **Take control of what you can.** Feeling powerless drives stress (NHS).
- **Connect with people.** Talking with friends, family or colleagues helps you see problems differently (NHS, WHO).
- **Make time for yourself**, deliberately (NHS).
- **Avoid unhealthy coping habits.** Alcohol, smoking and caffeine give only temporary relief (NHS).
- **Prioritise sleep** (WHO). See [Sleep basics](sleep-basics.md).
- **Limit news and social media** if they raise your stress (WHO).
- **Accept what you can't change**, and put your energy where you can make a difference (NHS).
- **Notice the good.** Write down a few things that went well each day (NHS).

## Using this in conversation

Don't hand over the whole list. Ask what's driving the stress, then pick one item that fits:

| They say | Try |
|---|---|
| "Everything's out of control" | Take control of one thing: name the single next action |
| "I've stopped seeing anyone" | Connect: one message to one person this week |
| "I'm wired every evening" | Wind-down routine, less news after dinner |
| "No time for me" | A protected 15 minutes on the calendar |
| "Can't stop worrying about X" | A worry list on paper, plus "what's in my control here?" |

## Movement: the guidelines, and a gentler way in

For adults, the CDC recommends **150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week** (or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mix of the two), plus **muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days a week**. WHO's fact sheet also states the 150-minute minimum.

These are population guidelines. For someone who's currently inactive, they can feel out of reach. The habit approach is to start far smaller and build. Any movement is better than none.

People with health conditions, pregnancy, injuries, disabilities, or symptoms such as chest pain or dizziness should check with their doctor before increasing activity.

## Tiny-steps worksheet (habits.md)

```
Real target (specific): <e.g. a 10-minute walk after lunch, weekdays>
Anchor (after an existing routine): <after I put my lunch dish away>
Make it easy: <shoes by the door; route planned>
Bad-day version: <walk to the end of the street and back — it counts>
Track: done-grid, reviewed weekly in check-in
Next step only after 2 steady weeks: <+5 minutes, or add Saturdays>
```

## Worked example

Priya feels "stressed and stuck at my desk". She agrees to a 10-minute walk after lunch on weekdays, anchored to putting her lunch dish away. Her bad-day version is a walk round the block.

- **Week 1:** 3 of 5 days. Wednesdays had back-to-back meetings.
- **Redesign:** on meeting-heavy days, walk at 5pm instead.
- **Week 3:** 5 of 5 days. She asks to add a Saturday walk with a friend. That covers movement and connection together.

No percentages and no streak talk, just "the design fits now".

## Limits of self-help

Suggest professional support (kindly, once) when:

- stress has gone on for weeks and is affecting work, relationships or sleep;
- there are physical symptoms (chest pain, palpitations, ongoing stomach problems). These need a doctor, whatever the cause;
- they're relying more on alcohol or other substances to cope;
- there's any crisis language. Go to the crisis guide immediately.

## Common mistakes

- Prescribing the full guideline amount to a beginner.
- Turning wellbeing into another to-do list.
- Calling a missed week a failure instead of a design problem.
- Giving medical or supplement advice. Never do this.
