# Check-in and journaling question bank

A good check-in question is specific, easy to answer and different from yesterday's. One question, a real response, at most one follow-up. Recent journal entries are the best source. The banks below are for when there's nothing recent to draw on, or for changing the angle.

## Choosing tonight's question

1. **Anything to follow up?** An event they mentioned, a worry, a plan: "How did the dentist go?"
2. **If not, rotate the angle.** Don't use the same type of question two nights in a row.
3. **Match the energy.** After heavy entries, ask something gentle. After good stretches, something forward-looking.
4. **Never open with a rating scale** unless they've asked to track numbers.

## Check-in bank (by angle)

**The day**
- What was the best five minutes of today?
- What took more energy than you expected?
- Anything you're glad is over?
- What surprised you today?

**Head-space**
- What's taking up the most room in your head tonight?
- Is there anything you'd like to put down before sleep?
- What's one thing you're looking forward to, even a small one?

**Body and energy**
- How's your energy been: steady, up and down, or running on fumes?
- Did you get outside at all today?
- What would make tomorrow feel a bit easier?

**People**
- Who did you enjoy talking to today?
- Anyone you've been meaning to get in touch with?

**Kindness to self**
- What did you handle well today, even if it was small?
- What would you tell a friend who'd had your day?

## Journaling prompts (deeper sessions)

Give one prompt at a time and let the answer guide the next one.

**Gratitude**
1. Name three specific things from this week that you're glad about.
2. For one of them: why did it land?
3. Who made it possible, and do they know?

**Processing a hard thing**
1. What happened? Just the facts, as if describing it to a stranger.
2. What did it stir up in you?
3. What do you think it needs from you now: action, acceptance, or a conversation?

**Decision**
1. What are the options, in your own words?
2. What's the fear behind each one?
3. What would you tell a friend in exactly this spot?
4. What would make this decision easier to undo, or easier to live with?

**Weekly or monthly review**
1. What gave you energy?
2. What drained you?
3. What's one small thing to try next week?

## Reflecting back well

- **Use their words.** "You called it 'the Sunday dread' again."
- **Show the evidence when you point out a pattern.** "That's the third entry this month where Sunday evening comes up."
- **Hold it lightly.** "Does that ring true, or am I reading too much in?" They own the interpretation.
- **Don't fix unless asked.** "Do you want ideas, or did you just want to say it out loud?"

## When a check-in turns heavy

- Weeks of exhaustion, hopelessness or flat mood in the entries: name it gently, once, and mention that a doctor or therapist can help with long stretches (see [Crisis and referral guide](crisis-and-referral-guide.md)).
- Any crisis language: stop the check-in flow and follow the crisis guide immediately.

## Journal entry format (wellness/journal/<date>.md)

```
<date> · <mode: check-in / gratitude / processing / decision / review>
Q: <the question asked>
<their words, lightly trimmed>
Context: <one line>
Commitments voiced: <if any>
```

## Worked example

Recent entries mention a job interview on Thursday. On Thursday evening, ask: "How did the interview go?" They say: "Fine, I think, but I fumbled the salary question." Reflect: "Fine overall, with one bumpy moment. That sounds like a lot of interviews." Follow up once: "Want to jot down what you'd say next time, while it's fresh?" Journal their answer and the commitment.

## Common mistakes

- Asking the same opener every night.
- Stacking three questions in one message.
- Turning a check-in into an assessment, or a list of tips.
- Commenting on missed days. Just open warmly next time.
