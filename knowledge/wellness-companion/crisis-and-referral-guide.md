# Crisis and referral guide

If anything suggests someone might be in danger, this takes priority over every other skill. Be warm and direct, take it seriously, and point to real help straight away. You aren't a crisis service, but you can be the bridge to one.

## Signs to respond to straight away

- Talk of suicide, wanting to die, or not wanting to be here
- Self-harm, now or planned
- Saying goodbye, giving things away, or "it won't matter soon"
- Describing being in danger from someone else
- Severe distress where they say they can't keep themselves safe

Don't wait for certain words. If you're unsure, ask gently and directly: "Are you thinking about ending your life?" Asking doesn't plant the idea. It shows you're taking them seriously.

## How to respond

1. **Acknowledge it warmly and plainly.** "I'm really glad you told me. That sounds incredibly heavy."
2. **Say you can't be enough on your own.** "I'm not able to give you the support you deserve right now, but people trained for this are available right now."
3. **Give the right contact for where they are** (below), as a clear next step.
4. **If life is at immediate risk**, the first step is local emergency services (911 in the US, 999 in the UK, 112 across the EU), or going to the nearest emergency department.
5. **Encourage a person nearby**: "Is there someone who can be with you right now?"
6. **Stay with them in the conversation.** Don't change the subject, lecture, or promise secrecy you can't keep.
7. **Afterwards**, gently check in next time without pressure, and keep encouraging professional support.

Don't do any of the following: argue about whether things are "that bad", give medical advice, minimise ("everyone feels like that"), or go back to habit tracking as though nothing happened.

## Where to point people (check that numbers are current)

| Where | Service |
|---|---|
| United States | **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline**: call or text 988, or chat at chat.988lifeline.org. Free, confidential, 24/7. |
| United Kingdom | **NHS 111**, choosing the mental health option, for urgent advice. **Samaritans**: 116 123. **Childline** (under 19): 0800 1111. 999 or A&E if life is at risk. |
| Anywhere else | **findahelpline.com** lists free, confidential helplines by country (more than 175 countries), by phone, text or chat. |

If you know the person's country from earlier conversations, give that country's service directly rather than a list.

## When to suggest a doctor or therapist (not an emergency)

Suggest it kindly, once, with a reason, then respect their choice. Raise it again only if things change.

- Low mood, hopelessness or loss of interest that has gone on for weeks
- Anxiety or stress that keeps getting in the way of work, school, relationships or sleep. WHO advises seeking care when stress symptoms persist and affect daily life.
- Sleep problems that have lasted months or that self-help hasn't fixed (see [Sleep basics](sleep-basics.md))
- Changes in eating, drinking or substance use that worry them
- Physical symptoms they're putting down to stress, which should be checked by a doctor anyway
- Grief that feels stuck, or a trauma they keep coming back to

Example wording: "You've mentioned feeling flat most days for a few weeks now. That's worth talking to a doctor or a therapist about. Not because something's wrong with you, but because that kind of stretch often gets easier with support. Want help working out how to book that?"

## Practical help you *can* give

- Help them write down what to say at a doctor's appointment
- Look up how to access therapy where they live (their own health service, workplace support schemes, community services)
- Draft a message to a friend asking for support
- Set a gentle reminder to make the call

## Recording

Journal entries record what the person said in their words, plus a note that support resources were shared. Don't write diagnoses or labels.

## Common mistakes

- Treating crisis language as a normal check-in topic.
- Giving a list of ten resources. Give one or two clear next steps.
- Suggesting therapy at every low moment. Ordinary bad days need a listener, not a referral.
- Forgetting that numbers differ by country.
