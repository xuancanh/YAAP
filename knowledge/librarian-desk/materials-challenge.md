# Handling a materials challenge

A challenge is a request to remove or restrict material. The safest response is boring: follow your written policy, every time, for every item. This doc is general. Laws on school and public library materials vary by country and US state and have changed often in recent years, so check current local law and your governing body's rules, and involve your director or legal counsel early.

## Concern versus formal challenge

- **Informal concern:** a patron tells staff they dislike an item. Listen, explain how materials are chosen, and offer the reconsideration form. Many concerns end here.
- **Formal request for reconsideration:** a written, signed form. This triggers the policy process.

Don't move, label or remove the item while it's being reviewed unless your policy says so. Quiet removal is itself a decision.

## The challenge-ready file

Have these ready before any challenge arrives:

- Collection development policy with selection criteria
- Reconsideration policy and form
- Board approval dates for both
- The library's statement on intellectual freedom (many adopt the ALA Library Bill of Rights or a national equivalent)
- For each item: reviews, awards, why it was selected (keep this light; a review or two is enough)
- Contacts: director, board chair, legal counsel, state library or national association support line

## The process, step by step

1. **Acknowledge** receipt in writing, within the time your policy sets. Thank them for using the process.
2. **Convene the review** named in policy (often a committee). Members read, view or listen to the whole work.
3. **Review against the selection criteria,** not against the complaint's excerpts or anyone's personal taste.
4. **Decide and write it up:** the decision, the criteria applied, and how to appeal.
5. **Respond to the complainant** respectfully and plainly.
6. **Record** the challenge and outcome. Many national associations track challenges; reporting is usually confidential.

## Talking to the complainant

- Listen fully; don't argue the book.
- Separate the person from the request. Most are parents worried about their own child.
- Explain that families decide for their own children; the library serves every family.
- Avoid promises ("we'll take it down") before the process runs.

## Drafting help this desk can give

- A plain-language acknowledgment letter
- A summary of the item's reviews and awards
- A one-page decision letter citing the policy section used
- A timeline of the case for the board

Legal questions (whether a new law applies, records requests, open-meeting rules) go to counsel.

## Common mistakes

- Pre-emptive self-censorship: not buying an item to avoid a challenge.
- Reviewing only the passages quoted in the complaint.
- Treating repeated challenges differently from first ones.
- Discussing the patron's identity outside the process.
