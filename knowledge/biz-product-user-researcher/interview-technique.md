# Interview technique

People are bad at predicting their own behaviour and eager to be helpful. A good interview asks about specific things they actually did and stays neutral enough that they can't tell what answer you hope for.

## Rewrite leading and speculative questions

| Avoid | Why | Ask instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use a feature that…?" | Hypothetical; people say yes to be kind | "Tell me about the last time you needed to…" |
| "How annoying is it when…?" | Assumes it's annoying | "How did that go?" |
| "Do you find exporting hard?" | Yes/no, suggests the answer | "Walk me through how you got the data out last month." |
| "What features do you want?" | Invites design by request | "What were you trying to get done when you hit that?" |
| "Why didn't you use the filter?" | Feels like blame | "What did you do at that point?" |
| "How often do you…?" | Estimates are unreliable | "When was the last time? And before that?" |
| Two questions in one | Only one gets answered | One question, then wait |

## Anchor on the last real occurrence

"Tell me about the last time you…" is the most useful sentence in an interview. Then stay in that story:

- What triggered it?
- What did you do first? Then?
- Which tools or people were involved?
- Where did it get stuck? What did you do about it?
- How did it end? What did it cost you (time, money, a missed deadline)?
- Have you tried to solve this another way? What happened?

The last question is a strong signal: people who have spent effort or money on a workaround have a real problem.

## Neutral probes

Keep a short list to use when an answer is thin:

- "Tell me more about that."
- "What do you mean by…?" (use their exact word)
- "Can you give me an example?"
- "What happened next?"
- "How did you feel about that?" (only after the facts)
- Silence. Count to five before speaking again.

## Session structure

1. **Intro and consent** (see [participant privacy](participant-privacy.md)): who you are, what it's for, recording, that there are no wrong answers, they can stop any time.
2. **Warm-up:** their role and a typical week.
3. **Main stories:** two or three recent occurrences of the behaviour you're studying.
4. **Concept or prototype (optional, last):** only after the stories, so it doesn't colour them.
5. **Wrap-up:** "Is there anything I should have asked?" and thanks.

## Things to avoid during the session

- **Pitching.** If you explain the product, the interview becomes a sales call.
- **Correcting them.** If they use it "wrong", that is data.
- **Accepting compliments as evidence.** "That sounds great" means nothing; ask what they did last time.
- **Filling silence.** Pauses often come before the most useful answer.

## Note-taking template

Separate observation from interpretation at the moment of writing; it saves hours in synthesis.

```
Participant: P<n>  Segment: <...>  Date: <...>  Consent: yes (recording yes/no)

| Time | Said / did (verbatim or close) | Context | My interpretation (marked) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 04:10 | "I export it and retype the totals into the finance sheet." | month-end | [I] workaround → data-out problem |

Workarounds:
Strong signals (spent time, money, or built something):
Surprises:
Follow-ups:
```

Use participant codes (P1, P2) from the start. Real names live only in the consent record, stored where the company allows.

## After each session

- Spend ten minutes writing the three things you learned while it's fresh.
- Note any guide questions that didn't work, and fix them before the next session.
- If the last two sessions added nothing new for the question, consider stopping recruitment.
