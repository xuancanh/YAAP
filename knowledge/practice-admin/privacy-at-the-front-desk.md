# Privacy at the front desk

General orientation only. The practice's privacy lead and its professional body decide specifics. Rules differ by country, and in the US some states add their own.

## The principle: minimum necessary

Share only what the person receiving the message needs for the purpose. In the US, the HIPAA Privacy Rule's "minimum necessary" standard says this for most uses and disclosures of protected health information. In the UK, health data is "special category data" under UK GDPR, which needs both a lawful basis and an extra condition, and data minimisation is a core principle. The same habit satisfies both: say less.

HHS guidance accepts that some incidental disclosure happens in a busy office (a name called in the waiting room, a sign-in sheet), as long as reasonable safeguards are in place and what's shown is limited.

## Applied to everyday admin

**Appointment reminders.** Name, date, time, practice name and a call-back number. Leave out the reason for the visit, test names and the practitioner's specialty if it reveals the condition (e.g. a named oncology clinic).

> Good: "Hi J, a reminder of your appointment at Riverside Practice on Tue 9 Mar at 10:30. Reply C to confirm or call 01234 567890."
> Avoid: "Reminder: your follow-up for your anxiety assessment…"

**Voicemail.** Same content as a reminder, no more. Use the method of contact the patient chose on intake.

**Sign-in.** Name and time only; no reason for visit. A clipboard where later patients read earlier names is a judgment call; many practices use a check-in tablet or a verbal check-in instead.

**Email and text.** Use the channel the patient agreed to. Don't reply-all; don't put health details in the subject line.

**Your own files.** Initials or reference codes, appointment-type labels only (e.g. "NP-60" for a 60-minute new-patient slot), never diagnoses or notes. The practice system is the record; this desk isn't.

**Phone callers.** Confirm identity per the practice's procedure before discussing anything, including whether someone is a patient.

## Public reviews

Never confirm that a reviewer is a patient or mention anything about their care in a public reply, even if they disclosed it themselves. Reply generally and move the conversation offline. See [the review template](fees-and-hard-messages.md#replying-to-a-public-review).

## Check before anything goes out

- Is any health information in it beyond an appointment-type label?
- Could someone who sees the phone screen or envelope learn something sensitive?
- Is it going through the channel the patient agreed to?
- Is the recipient the patient, or someone they've authorised in writing?
- Has the practitioner approved this wording?

## When to escalate

Stop and send to the practitioner or privacy lead:

- a message went to the wrong person or number (possible breach; there may be reporting duties and deadlines);
- a request for records from a lawyer, insurer, employer or family member;
- police or court requests;
- a patient asking to see, correct or delete their data;
- anything involving a minor or a safeguarding concern.

## Common mistakes

- Reasons for visit in reminder texts because "it helps them remember".
- Spreadsheets with full names and visit types emailed around.
- Discussing a patient at the front desk within earshot of the waiting room.
- Replying to a bad review with details of the visit.
