# Retrieval and spaced practice

Two of the most robust findings in learning research are that **pulling information out of memory strengthens it more than re-reading it**, and that **practice spread out over time beats the same practice crammed together**. A widely cited review (Dunlosky and colleagues, 2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice as the highest-utility study techniques it examined. Highlighting and re-reading rated low.

## Retrieval practice: what it looks like

The student produces the answer from memory before seeing it. Low stakes, quick, and often.

Formats that work in a one-to-one session:

- **Brain dump.** "Two minutes: write everything you remember about photosynthesis." Then compare it with the notes together.
- **Three from before.** Three short questions: one from last session, one from two sessions ago, and one from a month ago.
- **Blank-page method.** Draw the diagram, write the formula, or sketch the timeline from memory.
- **Explain it to me.** The student teaches back a step or a concept. Gaps show quickly.
- **Flashcards with a rule.** Answer out loud *before* flipping the card. Cards answered correctly move to longer gaps.
- **Past-paper questions** on topics from weeks ago, for exam students.

Keep it feeling like a game, not a test. Mistakes are useful information, and you correct them straight away.

## Spacing: a simple schedule

You don't need exact intervals. The principle is to come back to a topic after a gap, with the gap growing as it becomes secure.

| When the topic was taught | Revisit |
|---|---|
| Today | Homework within a few days |
| Next session | Opener question |
| Two to three sessions later | Opener or mixed practice |
| A month later | Mixed review set |
| Before the exam | Past-paper practice |

Track this in the student file: for each topic, the date taught and the dates retrieved, with a result (✔ secure, ~ shaky, ✘ forgot). Shaky topics come back sooner.

## Interleaving (mixing problem types)

Once a student can do each type on its own, mix them: a set with fractions, percentages and ratio questions in random order. The student then has to work out *which* method to use, and that's the skill exams test. Students often feel less fluent with mixed practice even when it's working, so tell them this in advance.

## Homework that uses this

- Make it short: 10–20 minutes for most ages.
- Use a mix: mostly today's objective, plus two or three questions from older topics.
- Make it checkable in the first minutes of the next session.
- Prefer "answer without notes, then check" over "copy up your notes".

## Worked example

Maya, Year 9 maths. The session opener for 4 November:

1. "Simplify 18/24." (Taught 21 October.)
2. "Expand 3(x − 4)." (Taught 14 October.)
3. "What's 15% of 60?" (Taught in September; marked shaky in the file.)

She gets 1 and 2 right, and for 3 she says "6", using 10% instead of 15%. That takes four minutes. Percentages go back into Thursday's homework, and the opener the following week includes another percentage question.

## Common mistakes

- Opening with a re-explanation instead of a question. Ask first, then explain.
- Only reviewing last week's topic. That's practice, not spacing.
- Making retrieval high-stakes. Anxiety blocks recall.
- Cramming the week before the exam *instead of* spacing. Cramming alone fades fast.
- Letting the student re-read notes and call it revision. Turn the notes into questions.
