# Coaching boundaries and referral guide

Coaching works with capable people moving towards goals. When the conversation is mostly about pain, the past, or symptoms that interfere with daily life, it's time to refer. Professional ethics codes, such as the ICF Code of Ethics, expect coaches to recognise the limits of their competence and refer when appropriate (checked 2026-11).

## Signs a referral may be needed

- Persistent low mood, hopelessness, or loss of interest lasting weeks.
- Anxiety or panic that stops the client functioning.
- Mentions of trauma, abuse, or grief that dominate sessions.
- Substance use that's affecting work or health.
- Sleep, appetite or concentration changes the client describes as unusual.
- Sessions that repeatedly turn into processing distress rather than moving forward.

These aren't diagnoses. They're signals that a licensed professional (doctor, psychologist, counsellor) is the right person.

## Immediate risk

If a client talks about suicide, self-harm, or harming others, or is in danger:

1. Take it seriously and stay calm; ask directly whether they're safe right now.
2. Encourage immediate contact with emergency services or a crisis line. In the US, call or text **988**; in the UK and Ireland, Samaritans on **116 123**; elsewhere, find a local line at findahelpline.com.
3. If danger is immediate, call emergency services.
4. Record what happened and what you did, factually.

Confidentiality has limits where there's serious risk of harm. These limits should be in the coaching agreement from the start.

## Drafting a warm referral (for the coach to adapt)

> "I've noticed that in our last few sessions a lot of what you've shared is about how heavy things feel, not just at work. That matters, and I think you deserve support that's set up for it, from someone trained in this. Would you be open to talking to your doctor or a counsellor? I'm happy to keep coaching alongside that, if it helps, or to pause until you're ready."

Key moves: name what you've noticed, normalise getting help, give a concrete next step, and say what happens to the coaching. Employee assistance programmes, the client's doctor, and national psychological associations' directories are common routes; which exist depends on the country and employer.

## Confidentiality with sponsors

- Agree in writing at the start: what the sponsor receives (often attendance and progress against goals), what they don't (session content), and the exceptions (serious risk).
- Three-way meetings share only what the client is ready to share.
- If the sponsor asks for more, the answer is "That's between the client and me; let me ask if she'd like to share it."

## Conflicts of interest

Watch for: coaching two people in a reporting line, being asked to assess the client for promotion or exit, or personal relationships with the client. Disclose and discuss; decline where it can't be managed.

## Common mistakes

- Continuing to "coach through" what is clearly a mental-health struggle.
- A cold referral that feels like rejection.
- Vague confidentiality terms discovered only when a sponsor asks.
