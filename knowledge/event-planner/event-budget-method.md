# Event budget method

Set the ceiling first, then split it. A budget built up from wish lists always comes out too high.

## Step 1: ceiling and per-head

Agree the total the host is comfortable spending, then divide by expected guests. The per-head figure makes trade-offs concrete: "Adding ten guests costs about the same as the band."

## Step 2: split by category

Start with the host's priorities. Rank what matters most (food? venue? photos?) and give those a larger share. A typical category list:

- venue and rentals
- food and drink (usually the largest line for a party with a meal)
- entertainment and music
- photography or video
- decor and flowers
- invitations, stationery, favours
- attire and beauty (weddings, formal events)
- transport and accommodation for guests or the host
- **contingency** (keep a reserve, commonly around 10%, for overruns and surprises)

There is no correct split; local prices vary widely. Research current prices for the event's location and note the date of each estimate.

## Step 3: the tracker

```
| Category | Budget | Quote | Booked with | Deposit paid (date) | Balance due (date) | Actual | Notes |
```

Update it every time money moves. Two dates matter most: when each balance is due, and the cancellation deadline after which deposits are lost.

## Hidden costs to ask about

- service charges, gratuities, and taxes on top of quoted prices;
- corkage, cake-cutting or outside-vendor fees at venues;
- overtime if the event runs late;
- delivery, setup and collection fees for rentals;
- minimum spends on private rooms;
- staffing for bars and cloakrooms;
- damage deposits, cleaning fees;
- postage for invitations and thank-yous.

## Cutting without ruining it

Cut in this order, checking with the host's priority list:

1. **Guest count.** The biggest lever for most events.
2. **Day and time.** Off-peak days, lunch instead of dinner, or off-season dates often cost less.
3. **Format.** Buffet or family-style vs. plated; a cocktail party instead of a full meal.
4. **Low-impact items.** Favours, printed menus, elaborate stationery.
5. **DIY with care.** Only where there are willing helpers and time; DIY that falls on the host the day before is a false saving.

Protect what the host said matters most, and food quality and safety.

## Worked example

Ceiling 3,000 for 40 guests: 75 per head. The host ranks food first, photos second. Draft split: food and drink 1,500; venue 450 (restaurant room with minimum spend counted in food); photographer 400; cake 150; decor 150; music (playlist + speaker hire) 50; contingency 300. The caterer adds a service charge not in the first quote, so the contingency absorbs it and the favours are dropped.

## Common mistakes

- No contingency, so the first surprise breaks the budget.
- Comparing quotes that include different things (one with service and tax, one without).
- Forgetting balances due before the event and the cancellation terms.
- Tracking quotes but not actuals.
