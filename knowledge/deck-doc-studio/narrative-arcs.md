# Narrative arcs

A deck is an argument. Decide the argument's shape and write it as a sequence of sentences before making a single slide.

## Start with four questions

1. Who's in the room, and what do they already believe?
2. What must they believe or decide by the end?
3. What will they object to?
4. How long is the slot, really?

If there's no decision or change of mind at stake, it may be a document, not a deck. Say so.

## Three arcs

**Answer first (pyramid)**

Lead with the conclusion, then the reasons, then evidence for each. This comes from Barbara Minto's pyramid principle and suits senior audiences who want the answer and will ask for detail.

```
1. Recommendation: we should X.
2. Because reason A.
3. Because reason B.
4. Because reason C.
5. What we need from you / next steps.
```

**Situation, complication, resolution**

Set up the shared starting point, introduce the problem, then present the way forward. Good for persuading people who don't yet see the problem.

```
1. Situation: where we are (agreed facts).
2. Complication: what changed or what's at risk.
3. Question: so what should we do?
4. Resolution: the proposal.
5. Evidence and plan.
```

**Guided tour**

A logical walk through a topic, step by step. Good for teaching, onboarding or a project update where nothing is contested.

```
1. What this covers.
2. Part 1 → takeaway.
3. Part 2 → takeaway.
4. What it means for you.
```

## Choosing

| Audience and goal | Arc |
|---|---|
| Executives, decision needed, limited time | Answer first |
| Need to persuade; problem not yet accepted | Situation, complication, resolution |
| Teaching or status, no debate | Guided tour |
| Bad news | Answer first, with the plan straight after |

## Write the storyline

Write one sentence per slide, in order, as a plain list. Then read it aloud. If the sentences alone don't make the case, more slides won't fix it.

Check:

- Does each sentence follow from the one before?
- Is any sentence a topic ("Market overview") rather than a point ("The market is growing, but only in the mid-tier")? Rewrite.
- Could you cut a sentence without losing the argument? Cut it or move it to the appendix.
- Does the main line fit in about two-thirds of the slot, leaving time for questions?

## Worked example

Goal: get approval to hire two support staff. Audience: the leadership team.

Draft storyline (topic titles): "Support overview. Ticket trends. Team capacity. Options. Ask."

Rewritten, answer first:

1. We need two more support hires this quarter to hold response times.
2. Ticket volume has grown faster than the team since the spring launch.
3. Response times have slipped past our target in each of the last three months.
4. Automation and self-help can absorb some growth, but not all of it.
5. Two hires restore the target; the cost is offset by fewer refunds and churn (show the estimate and its assumptions).
6. Decision needed today: approve two roles.

Each sentence becomes a slide title. The evidence on each slide supports that sentence and nothing else.

## Common mistakes

- Building slides before the storyline exists.
- Chronological order ("first we did, then we found…") when the audience wants the answer.
- Hiding the ask at the end of a long deck.
- Putting every analysis in the main line instead of the appendix.
