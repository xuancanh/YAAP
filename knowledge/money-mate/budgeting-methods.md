# Budgeting methods

There's no best method, only the one the person will keep using. Start from what they actually spent over the last two or three months, then pick a structure.

## The main methods

| Method | How it works | Suits | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Zero-based** | Every unit of income gets a job (bills, spending, saving) until nothing is unassigned | Detail-minded people; tight months | Takes effort monthly; can feel strict |
| **50/30/20** | A rule of thumb: about half of take-home pay to needs, about 30% to wants, about 20% to savings and extra debt payments | People who want a simple shape | Housing costs in many cities make "needs" far above half. Treat as a starting shape, not a target |
| **Envelopes** (cash or digital) | Fixed amounts into categories; when an envelope is empty, spending stops | Overspending in a few categories (food, fun) | Needs a rule for moving money between envelopes |
| **Pay yourself first** | Savings move out on payday; spend the rest freely, bills covered | People who hate tracking | Doesn't catch slow overspending |
| **Two-account** | Bills account (fixed costs, automated) + spending account (the rest) | Couples, people with irregular attention | Must size the bills account correctly, including annual bills |

Methods mix well. A common combination: pay yourself first, a bills account, and an envelope for the one category that leaks.

## Building it from actuals

1. **Gather two or three months** of statements (CSV is easiest).
2. **Categorise** every transaction. Keep categories few: housing, utilities, groceries, transport, insurance, debt payments, subscriptions, eating out, shopping, kids, health, gifts, other.
3. **Find the irregulars.** Annual and quarterly bills (insurance, car tax, subscriptions, school costs) divided into monthly amounts.
4. **Average** each category. Note the spread: food might be steady; "shopping" might swing wildly.
5. **Compare** to take-home income. That gap, positive or negative, is the starting point.
6. **Pick a method** and set amounts slightly above current actuals in hard-to-cut areas. A budget that's too tight in month one gets abandoned.

## Worked example

Monthly take-home: 3,000 (any currency). Three-month averages:

| Category | Actual |
|---|---|
| Rent | 1,200 |
| Utilities and phone | 180 |
| Groceries | 450 |
| Transport | 200 |
| Insurance (annual 600 ÷ 12) | 50 |
| Subscriptions | 70 |
| Eating out | 300 |
| Shopping and other | 350 |
| Saving | 200 |
| **Total** | **3,000** |

Observations: needs (rent through insurance) are about 2,080, around 69% of income, well above the 50/30/20 shape. That's normal for high-rent areas; it doesn't mean failure. The flexible categories are eating out and shopping (650). The user decides whether to move, say, 100 of that into saving. Make it one change, then review next month.

## Common mistakes

- Budgeting from what you *wish* you spent.
- Forgetting annual bills, then calling them "unexpected".
- Too many categories to keep up with.
- Treating a rule of thumb as a pass/fail test.
- Changing everything at once. One adjustment per month sticks.
