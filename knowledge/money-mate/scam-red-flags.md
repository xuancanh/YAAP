# Scam red flags

When the user forwards a message, describes a call or asks "is this legit?", work through the four signs. Scams are designed to rush people; slowing down is most of the defence.

## The four signs (US FTC)

1. **They pretend to be from an organisation you know:** a government agency, bank, utility, delivery company, tech firm or charity. Caller ID and email names can be faked.
2. **They say there's a problem or a prize:** you owe money, an account is locked, a family member is in trouble, a virus is on your computer, or you've won something but must pay a fee.
3. **They pressure you to act now:** don't hang up, don't tell anyone, threats of arrest, lawsuits, deportation or losing a licence.
4. **They tell you to pay in an unusual way:** gift cards, wire transfer, payment apps, cryptocurrency, or "deposit this cheque and send some back".

Any one sign is reason to stop. Two or more is almost certainly a scam.

## Common household scams

| Scam | How it looks |
|---|---|
| Bank impersonation | "Fraud team" calls, says your account is at risk, asks you to move money to a "safe account" |
| Delivery or toll text | A small fee to release a parcel or pay a toll, via a link |
| Family emergency | A message from a "new number": "Hi Mum, I lost my phone, can you pay this bill?" |
| Tax or government | Threats of arrest unless you pay now |
| Investment or crypto | Guaranteed high returns, often met through social media or dating apps |
| Purchase scams | Cheap goods or rentals online, pay by bank transfer, nothing arrives |
| Tech support | Pop-up or call saying your computer is infected; asks for remote access |
| Invoice redirection | An email, apparently from a builder or solicitor, saying bank details have changed |

## What to do in the moment

- **Stop.** Don't click, reply, pay or share codes.
- **Check independently.** Contact the organisation using a number or website you look up yourself, not one in the message.
- **Never share one-time passcodes.** Banks don't ask for them.
- **Verify "new number" family messages** by calling the old number or asking a question only they'd know.
- **Changed bank details?** Confirm by phone on a number you already have, before paying.
- **Talk to someone you trust.** Scammers rely on secrecy.

## If money has already gone

1. **Contact the bank or payment provider immediately,** using the number on the card or the official app. Speed matters for recovering payments.
2. **Change passwords** for any account the scammer may know about; turn on two-factor authentication.
3. **Report it.** In the US: ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Elsewhere: the national fraud-reporting service and the police. Reporting helps even if money isn't recovered.
4. **Watch for follow-up scams.** "Recovery" services that promise to get money back for a fee are often scammers too.
5. **Keep records:** messages, numbers, transaction details, dates.

Note: protections and refund rules differ by country and payment method. The bank can explain what applies.

## Common mistakes

- Trusting caller ID or a familiar logo.
- Believing a real-looking website is real; check the address carefully.
- Feeling too embarrassed to report. Scams fool careful people.
