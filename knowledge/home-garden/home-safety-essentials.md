# Home safety essentials

Check this list before giving any DIY guidance. Emergency numbers and legal rules differ by country; use the user's local ones.

## If they smell gas

Utility and government guidance agrees on the core steps:

1. **Get everyone out.** Don't stay to investigate.
2. **No sparks.** Don't switch lights or appliances on or off, don't use flames, and don't smoke.
3. **Open doors and windows on the way out** if it's quick and safe.
4. **Call from outside,** at a safe distance, to the gas emergency line or emergency services for their country (for example, 0800 111 999 is the UK's national gas emergency number; in the US, call 911 or the gas utility).
5. **Don't go back in** until the emergency service says it's safe.

Whether the user should turn off the gas at the meter varies by utility; follow local instructions.

## Carbon monoxide (CO)

CO has no smell. Symptoms like headache, dizziness and nausea that ease when away from home are a warning sign.

- The US Consumer Product Safety Commission advises CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas.
- Fuel-burning appliances (boilers, gas fires, wood stoves) should be serviced by a qualified engineer on the maker's schedule.
- If a CO alarm sounds: get out into fresh air, call emergency services, and get anyone with symptoms medical help.

## Smoke alarms

The US Fire Administration advises:

- alarms inside and outside each bedroom and sleeping area, and on every level including the basement;
- **test monthly;**
- replace the whole alarm 10 years from its manufacture date (sealed 10-year units: follow the maker's instructions).

Other countries' rules on number and type (linked, hard-wired) differ; landlords often have legal duties.

## Electrical limits

- Safe for most people: changing a bulb, resetting a tripped breaker once, replacing a plug fuse, testing an RCD/GFCI with its test button.
- Always a qualified electrician: anything at the consumer unit or panel, new circuits or sockets, bathroom and outdoor wiring, and any burning smell, scorch marks or repeated tripping.
- Many countries legally restrict who may do certain electrical work. Check before suggesting DIY.

## Ladders

The UK Health and Safety Executive's guidance for leaning ladders:

- set at about 75°, the "1 in 4" rule: one unit out from the wall for every four units up;
- on firm, level ground, with the top resting against something solid;
- keep three points of contact and your belt buckle between the stiles;
- don't overreach; move the ladder instead.

Roof work and anything at height for long periods goes to a pro with proper access equipment.

## Asbestos and lead paint in older homes

- **Asbestos.** The HSE warns that buildings built or refurbished before 2000 may contain asbestos (in the UK; other countries' dates differ). Common spots include textured ceilings, old floor tiles, pipe lagging and cement roof sheets. If it's intact, leave it alone. Don't drill, sand or cut suspected material; get it tested and use licensed professionals for removal.
- **Lead paint.** In the US, homes built before 1978 may contain lead-based paint. The EPA requires paid contractors disturbing paint in those homes to be lead-safe certified. Homeowners doing their own work should use lead-safe practices, and take extra care with children and pregnant people around.

## Common mistakes

- Investigating a gas smell instead of leaving.
- Disabling a smoke alarm while cooking and forgetting to re-enable it.
- Sanding old paint or drilling textured ceilings without checking first.
- Treating a breaker that keeps tripping as a nuisance instead of a symptom.
