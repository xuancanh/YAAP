# Record gaps and substitutes

Every country has holes in its records: fires, wars, floods, deliberate destruction, or records that were simply never made. Good research names the gap early and goes straight to the records that survive around it.

## Two well-known examples

These are well documented; check the cited archives for current details and newly recovered fragments.

**United States, 1890 census.** Most of the 1890 population schedules were damaged or destroyed after a fire in the Commerce Building in Washington, D.C., in January 1921, according to the Census Bureau. The National Archives' own history shows the losses were more complicated than "burned": the damaged remainder was later authorised for destruction. Fragments survive, as do many of the special 1890 schedules of Union veterans and widows.

**Ireland, 19th-century censuses.** The National Archives of Ireland explains that the 1821-1851 returns were largely lost in the 1922 fire at the Public Record Office in the Four Courts, with only fragments surviving; the 1861 and 1871 returns were destroyed by government order soon after they were taken; and 1881 and 1891 were pulped during the First World War. The 1901 and 1911 censuses survive. Projects to reconstruct lost Irish records continue, so re-check what has come online.

## Substitute record types

When the obvious record is gone, these often place a person in time and place. Not all exist everywhere; check the FamilySearch Research Wiki page for the specific country, region and era.

| Gap | Try instead |
|---|---|
| Missing census | Tax and rate books, land valuations, city directories, electoral registers, school records, state or local censuses |
| No civil registration yet | Church registers (all denominations nearby), bishop's transcripts, cemetery and burial records, gravestones |
| Lost church registers | Neighboring parishes, nonconformist chapels, marriage banns/licences, newspapers' notices |
| Unknown village of origin | Naturalisation papers, passenger lists, military draft cards, obituaries, the siblings' and neighbors' records (cluster research) |
| No death record | Probate and wills, funeral home records, pension files, newspaper notices |

## Planning around a gap

1. **Name the gap.** "Ireland, 1850s: no surviving census for this county."
2. **List what the missing record would have told you.** Household members, ages, place.
3. **Map each item to a surviving record type** that also carries it.
4. **Order by cost.** Free online indexes first, then paid sites, then letters to archives, then visits.
5. **Log the gap in the wall file** so nobody re-searches the missing record.

## Common mistakes

- Spending months searching for a record that doesn't exist.
- Assuming "not online" means "doesn't exist". Many records are only on microfilm, in local archives, or held by churches.
- Believing a famous loss is total. Fragments and duplicates often survive; check the archive's own guide.
- Forgetting boundary changes. The parish or county may have changed names or countries, which changes where the records are now held.

## Worked example

A user's Irish ancestor, Patrick, emigrated around 1850 from "County Clare." No census survives. Substitutes: Griffith's Valuation (a property survey, mid-19th century) for Clare households of the surname, Catholic parish registers for baptisms of siblings, and the US naturalisation file of his brother, which names the townland. The townland then unlocks the parish register search. The plan is logged with each step's expected payoff.
