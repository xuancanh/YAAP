# Evidence and proof

Genealogists judge records on three separate questions. Keeping them apart is what stops a family legend from becoming a "fact" in the tree.

## Three ways to grade a record

**1. The source: original or derivative?**
- *Original:* the first recording, such as the parish register page or the signed certificate.
- *Derivative:* a copy, transcription, abstract or index made from it. Indexes are finding aids; go to the image whenever you can.
- *Authored work:* a published family history or someone's online tree. A pointer to sources, not a source itself.

**2. The information: primary or secondary?** Judge each fact separately.
- *Primary:* given by someone who witnessed the event. The doctor on a death certificate for the date of death.
- *Secondary:* given second-hand. The same certificate's birth date and parents' names, supplied by a grieving relative decades later.
- *Undetermined:* you can't tell who gave it (common in censuses).

**3. The evidence: direct, indirect or negative?**
- *Direct:* answers the question by itself ("John, son of Mary and Peter, baptised 3 May 1841").
- *Indirect:* answers only when combined with other facts (an age in three censuses pointing to a birth year).
- *Negative:* an expected record is absent, which can itself tell you something (no burial in the parish, so maybe they moved).

A death certificate is one original source with a mix of primary and secondary information. That's why its death date is usually stronger than its birth date.

## The Genealogical Proof Standard

The proof standard, set out by the Board for Certification of Genealogists, has five parts. In plain words:

1. **Search reasonably exhaustively.** Look in every record type likely to hold the answer for that time and place, not just the first hit.
2. **Cite completely.** Someone else should be able to find the same record.
3. **Analyse and correlate.** Compare what each record says and how reliable each fact is.
4. **Resolve conflicts.** Don't ignore the record that disagrees; explain it.
5. **Write the conclusion.** A short reasoned statement of why the answer holds.

A conclusion that meets all five can be called "proved". Anything less is "probable" or "possible". Use those words in the person file.

## Resolving a conflict

1. List each record's claim side by side with its grade (original/derivative, primary/secondary).
2. Ask who gave the information and how close they were to the event.
3. Look for a reason for the error: rounding ages, a clerk's mishearing, someone hiding an age at marriage.
4. Find an independent third record if the first two can't be reconciled.
5. Record the reasoning in the person file, even if the answer stays open.

## Worked example

Question: when was Anna Kowalski born?

- 1900 census (US): born March 1872, information from an unknown household member.
- 1920 census: age 46, so about 1873-74.
- Death certificate (1935): born 12 March 1871, informant her son.
- Baptism register, home parish (found after the village was identified): baptised 14 March 1871, two days after birth.

The baptism is an original record with primary information made at the time, and it agrees with the death certificate's day and month. Conclusion: born 12 March 1871, with the census years explained as normal age drift. Each record is cited in her file, and the conflict note stays in.

## Common mistakes

- Treating an online tree or index as proof.
- Giving one record's birth date the same weight as all the others.
- Merging two people with the same name because the dates are close.
- Deleting the record that "doesn't fit" instead of explaining it.

When stakes are high (legal inheritance, citizenship by descent, lineage society applications), refer the user to a credentialed genealogist or the body's own rules of evidence.
