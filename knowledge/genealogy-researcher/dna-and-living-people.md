# DNA and living people

DNA testing is a powerful source, but it's still a source. It needs the same analysis as a parish register, and it touches living people, so it needs more care.

## Which test answers which question

| Test | What it follows | Useful for |
|---|---|---|
| Autosomal | DNA from all ancestral lines, diluting each generation | Finding cousins on any line within roughly the last several generations; checking a proposed relationship |
| Y-DNA | Father-to-son line only (tested by males) | Surname studies; testing whether two men share a paternal line |
| Mitochondrial (mtDNA) | Mother-to-child line | Deep maternal line questions; occasionally confirming a specific maternal link |

Most hobbyist tests are autosomal. "Ethnicity estimates" are statistical guesses that change as companies update their reference panels. Don't use them as evidence for a specific ancestor.

## Reading a match

- The amount of shared DNA suggests a *range* of possible relationships, not one. A given amount could mean a half-sibling, grandparent or uncle, for example.
- Use the testing company's or ISOGG's reference tools to see the range, then use the paper trail and ages to narrow it.
- A match proves that two people are related; the paper tree says how. You usually need both.
- Shared matches (people who match both of you) help sort matches onto the right side of the family.

## Living people

- Keep living people's details out of anything shared or published: name and relationship only, marked *living*.
- Don't reveal a match's identity, results or private tree to others without their permission.
- Ask before testing someone, and explain what could come out (see below). Testing a minor or someone who can't consent needs extra caution; the person's guardian decides.
- Respect testing companies' terms and privacy settings, which differ and change.

## Unexpected discoveries

DNA can reveal unknown siblings, adoptions, donor conception or a different biological parent. The results may upset several people at once.

1. **Pause before telling anyone.** Check the evidence twice; is there an innocent explanation (a mis-labelled kit, a relative tested twice)?
2. **Decide with the user** who needs to know, in what order, and whether at all.
3. **Never contact a match about a sensitive finding on the user's behalf without their clear instruction.**
4. **Suggest support.** Many people find a counselor, a support group for the specific situation (such as adoption or donor conception), or a trusted friend helpful. If the user seems distressed, say so kindly and point them to support.

## Common mistakes

- Treating ethnicity percentages as proof of a specific ancestor.
- Picking the most exciting relationship from a match's range instead of the most likely.
- Uploading a relative's results to another site without their agreement.
- Putting living cousins' names into a public tree.

## Worked example

A user matches a stranger with an amount of DNA consistent with several relationships, including half-sibling or uncle. The user's age and the match's age rule some out. Shared matches all sit on the user's father's side. The agent sets out the remaining options and the paper records that could tell them apart, flags that this may be sensitive, and asks the user how they want to proceed before drafting any message to the match.
