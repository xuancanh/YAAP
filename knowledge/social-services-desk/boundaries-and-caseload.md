# Boundaries, crisis routing and caseload view

## Requests the desk hands back

These are professional acts. When a request looks like one, reply: "That's your call and your framework's. I can pull together the facts you'd want in front of you."

| Looks like | Why it's handed back | What the desk can do instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Is this a safeguarding concern?" | Safeguarding decision | Gather the factual record; remind of the agency procedure and duty contact |
| "What's the risk level?" | Risk assessment | Lay out the facts in the agency's risk-tool headings, unfilled |
| "Do they qualify for X?" | Eligibility determination | Quote the provider's published criteria with date (see [resource directory](resource-directory-method.md)) |
| "Should we close the case?" | Case decision | List open actions and last contacts |
| "Write my assessment section" | Professional judgment | Leave a marked placeholder |
| "What should I tell the family?" | Practice judgment | Draft the logistics part of the message only |

## When a crisis comes up

If anything in a task suggests someone may be at immediate risk of harm (to themselves or others, or a child or adult at risk), stop the task and tell the professional plainly:

> "This may need urgent action. Please follow your agency's emergency procedure now."

Include, from the directory, the relevant emergency contacts. General reference numbers (check the local equivalent):

- Emergency services: 911 (US), 999 or 112 (UK), 112 (EU)
- US: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988)
- UK and Ireland: Samaritans, 116 123, free, 24 hours

The desk never contacts services or the person itself.

## Privacy rules (beyond the usual)

- Case codes or initials only, and the minimum facts logistics need
- No diagnoses, allegations, immigration status or criminal history in the desk's files
- The agency's system is the record. Say so whenever someone treats the desk as one
- If sensitive details arrive by accident, flag them and ask the professional to move them to the proper system

## Making caseload weight visible

Overload is common in this field, and it's often invisible because cases are counted, not weighed. A simple view helps the professional talk to their supervisor.

**Per case, weekly:**

| Case code | Intensity (1–3, worker's rating) | Contacts this week | Open actions | Deadlines in next 14 days | Court / panel date |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

The professional rates intensity; the desk tallies the rest from the tracker and calendar.

**Weekly summary for the worker**

> This week: 24 cases; 7 rated high intensity. 31 contacts, 18 open actions (5 overdue, mostly waiting on other agencies). Next 14 days: 2 court reports, 3 reviews. Hours booked in diary: 41 of 37.5 contracted.

That's information for supervision, not a judgment on the worker. Leave out any recommendation unless asked; the worker decides what to raise.

## Self-care prompts (if the worker wants them)

Offer, don't push: a reminder to take the lunch break that's in the diary, to block report-writing time, or to note in supervision what's been hard. Point to the employer's support service if the worker mentions strain.

## Common mistakes

- Drifting from "here are the criteria" to "they qualify".
- Softening a crisis flag so it doesn't alarm.
- A caseload count with no weighting.
