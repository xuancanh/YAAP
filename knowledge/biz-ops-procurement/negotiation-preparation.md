# Negotiation preparation

Most of a negotiation's outcome is decided before it starts, by how well the alternative is developed and how clearly the asks are ranked. You write the brief; a human leads the conversation and sends any offer.

## Start with our alternative

The BATNA, the "best alternative to a negotiated agreement" (a term from Fisher and Ury's *Getting to Yes*), is what we will do if no deal is reached. It sets the walk-away: any deal worse than the alternative should be refused.

For a renewal, list the real alternatives and cost each one with the [total cost method](total-cost-and-scoring.md):

- Switch to a named competitor (with a quote, not a guess).
- Downgrade to a lower tier or fewer seats.
- Use a tool we already have.
- Stop using the capability.

The alternative is only as strong as it is real. A competitor nobody has trialled is weak leverage; a completed trial and a quote are strong. If there is time before the notice date, strengthening the alternative is often the most valuable preparation.

## Understand the vendor's side

Write down what the vendor likely wants, because a good deal gives them something they value that costs us little:

- Longer commitment or multi-year term.
- Predictable payment (annual upfront).
- Growth in seats or a product add-on.
- A reference, case study or logo use (only if company policy allows).
- Timing: deals closed in their reporting period. Note it as a possibility, not a fact, unless they have said so.

Also note their likely constraints: list-price policies, approval levels, standard terms they will not change.

## Rank the asks

Write each ask with three levels and the evidence behind it:

| Ask | Target | Acceptable | Walk-away | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price | | | | competitor quote, usage trend |
| Renewal uplift cap | | | | current terms uncapped |
| Seat flexibility | reduce at renewal | true-down once a year | no flexibility | 40% of seats unused |
| Term | 12 months | 24 months with cap | 36 months | |
| Exit / data export | | | | |

Put them in priority order. The human negotiator needs to know which ask to trade away first.

## Plan the concessions

List what we can offer, only where the company's policy allows it:

- Longer term in exchange for a price cap.
- Annual payment in exchange for a discount.
- A case study or reference.
- Consolidating another tool onto this vendor.

Pair each concession with what we want back. Never offer one without an ask attached; unpaired concessions just lower the price of the deal for the vendor.

## The brief

```
Vendor: <name>   Contract: <id>   Renewal: <date>   Notice deadline: <date>
Decision owner: <human>   Negotiator: <human>

Facts: current price and terms; usage trend (seats paid vs active); satisfaction from the owner.
Our alternative: <the best one, costed>; strength: <weak / moderate / strong> because <...>.
Vendor interests: <...>
Asks (ranked): <table above>
Concessions available: <paired with asks>
Opening position: <what to ask for first>
Walk-away: <the point at which we take the alternative>
Deadline risk: <what happens if talks pass the notice date>
```

Save to /org/operations/negotiations/<vendor>-<date>.md and request review.

## Pitfalls

- Starting talks too close to the notice deadline. Without time to switch, the alternative is not real, and the vendor will know.
- Setting the walk-away from what feels fair rather than from the alternative's cost.
- Revealing the walk-away or the budget in the brief's shared summary. Keep the brief internal.
- Bundling every ask as equally important. Rank them.
- Forgetting non-price terms: uplift caps, seat flexibility and exit terms often matter more over time than the headline discount.
