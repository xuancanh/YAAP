# Food cost math

Two numbers matter: what each plate should cost (plate cost) and what the kitchen actually spent on food for the sales it made (actual food-cost percentage). The gap between them is waste, theft, over-portioning or pricing drift.

## Plate cost

1. List every ingredient with the quantity used per plate, including garnish, oil, sauce and the side.
2. Use the **as-purchased (AP) price** from the latest invoice.
3. Apply the **yield**: the share of the purchased item that ends up usable after trimming, peeling, bones or cooking loss.
   **Edible-portion cost per unit = AP cost per unit ÷ yield.**
4. Sum the lines. Add a small allowance for things you can't measure per plate (salt, frying oil) if the owner wants.

**Measure your own yields.** Weigh a case before and after prep a few times. Published yield tables are a starting point, but your trim and your supplier's quality decide the real number.

### Worked plate cost (illustrative)

Salmon main:

| Item | Qty | AP price | Yield | EP cost/unit | Line cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Salmon side | 180 g | 24.00/kg | 80% | 30.00/kg | 5.40 |
| New potatoes | 200 g | 2.00/kg | 90% | 2.22/kg | 0.44 |
| Green beans | 80 g | 6.00/kg | 85% | 7.06/kg | 0.56 |
| Butter sauce | 40 ml | batch cost 8.00/L | — | 8.00/L | 0.32 |
| Lemon, herbs | — | | | | 0.25 |
| **Plate cost** | | | | | **6.97** |

At a menu price of 22.00 net of tax, the dish's food-cost percentage is 6.97 ÷ 22.00 ≈ 31.7%, and its cash margin is 15.03.

## Actual food-cost percentage (per period)

**Cost of goods sold (COGS) = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory**
**Food-cost % = food COGS ÷ food sales**

- Count inventory on the same day and time each period (e.g. Monday before delivery).
- Use the same prices for counting each time (latest invoice price is common).
- Keep food and beverage separate.
- Food sales net of tax, discounts and comps.

### Worked week (illustrative)

Opening 3,200 + purchases 5,600 − closing 3,000 = COGS 5,800. Food sales 17,500. Food cost = 5,800 ÷ 17,500 ≈ 33.1%.

## Theoretical vs actual

**Theoretical cost** = for every dish, units sold × plate cost, summed. If the menu sold 17,500 of food with a theoretical cost of 5,250 (30.0%), the actual 33.1% means about 550 of food is unaccounted for this week. Look at:

- **Waste:** is there a waste log? Over-prep on slow days?
- **Portioning:** are scoops and scales used?
- **Recipe drift:** cooks adding more of the expensive item.
- **Price changes:** an invoice went up and plate costs weren't updated.
- **Comps and staff meals** not rung through.
- **Receiving errors and theft:** short deliveries signed for; stock leaving.

## What a "good" percentage is

There isn't one universal target. It depends on concept, prices, labour model and location. Track your own trend week to week and focus on the gap to theoretical. If the owner wants a benchmark, use one from their accountant or a local trade body.

## Weekly routine

1. Count closing inventory.
2. Total the week's food invoices (credits deducted).
3. Compute COGS and food-cost %.
4. Compare to last four weeks and to theoretical.
5. Name the biggest likely cause and one action.

## Common mistakes

- Using AP cost without yield, which makes trimmed proteins look cheap.
- Counting inventory on different days, so deliveries distort the number.
- Including drinks in food cost.
- Judging dishes by percentage instead of cash margin (see [menu engineering](menu-engineering-method.md)).
