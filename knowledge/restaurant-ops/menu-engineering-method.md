# Menu engineering method

Menu engineering ranks each dish on two things: how much money it makes per sale (contribution margin) and how often it sells (popularity). The matrix comes from Kasavana and Smith's work in the 1980s and is still the standard approach.

## Data you need

For one menu category (mains, starters; don't mix them) over a representative period, such as four to eight weeks without a big holiday:

- units sold per dish (from the till export);
- menu price (net of sales tax/VAT);
- plate cost per dish (see [food cost math](food-cost-math.md)).

## The calculation

1. **Contribution margin (CM)** per dish = price − plate cost.
2. **Average CM** for the category = total CM of all units sold ÷ total units sold.
3. **Popularity threshold.** The classic method uses 70% of the average share: (1 ÷ number of dishes) × 0.7. A dish is "high popularity" if its share of units is at or above that.
4. Classify:

| | High CM (≥ average) | Low CM |
|---|---|---|
| **High popularity** | **Star** | **Plowhorse** |
| **Low popularity** | **Puzzle** | **Dog** |

## What to do with each

- **Stars:** keep them as they are. Give them the best spot on the menu; keep quality consistent; test a small price rise.
- **Plowhorses** (popular, low margin): lower the plate cost without the guest noticing (portion of the expensive component, garnish, a cheaper side), or raise the price a little. Don't feature them more.
- **Puzzles** (profitable, rarely ordered): find out why. Rename, re-describe, reposition, have staff recommend it, or lower the price slightly. If nothing works after a cycle, drop it.
- **Dogs:** remove or rework, unless it serves a purpose (the one vegan main, a regular's favourite that brings a table). Name the reason if you keep it.

## Worked example (illustrative numbers)

Five mains, eight weeks:

| Dish | Sold | Price | Cost | CM | Total CM |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Burger | 600 | 16 | 5.50 | 10.50 | 6,300 |
| Steak | 200 | 28 | 12.00 | 16.00 | 3,200 |
| Risotto | 120 | 18 | 4.00 | 14.00 | 1,680 |
| Fish | 330 | 22 | 9.50 | 12.50 | 4,125 |
| Chicken | 250 | 17 | 7.00 | 10.00 | 2,500 |
| **Total** | **1,500** | | | | **17,805** |

Average CM = 17,805 ÷ 1,500 = 11.87. Popularity threshold = (1 ÷ 5) × 0.7 = 14% of units = 210 sold.

- Burger: 600 sold (high), CM 10.50 (low) → **plowhorse**
- Steak: 200 sold (low), CM 16.00 (high) → **puzzle**
- Risotto: 120 (low), CM 14.00 (high) → **puzzle**
- Fish: 330 (high), CM 12.50 (high) → **star**
- Chicken: 250 (high), CM 10.00 (low) → **plowhorse**

Actions: feature the fish; try a 1.00 rise on the burger and check the bun and side cost; describe the risotto better and have servers recommend it; keep the steak but test its placement.

## Menu design levers

- The eye goes first to prominent positions; put stars and puzzles there, and box or highlight one or two.
- Remove currency symbols and trailing zeros where it suits the style; keep prices aligned to the description rather than in a column that invites price-scanning.
- Descriptions that name origin or technique help puzzles.
- Fewer choices per section usually sell more of the right ones.

## Common mistakes

- Mixing categories (a starter's margin isn't comparable to a main's).
- Using food-cost percentage instead of cash margin. A low-percentage dish can make less money per plate.
- Stale plate costs after supplier price changes.
- Changing everything at once, so you can't tell what worked. Change a few dishes, then re-run next quarter.
