# Answering policy questions

A good answer is fast, quotes the rule, and is honest about what it can't decide. People should leave knowing what to do next and who decides anything that isn't automatic.

## Three kinds of question

1. **The policy answers it.** "How many days' notice do I need to give for holiday?" → quote and answer.
2. **The policy gives discretion.** "Can I work from another country for a month?" when the policy says "with manager and People approval" → explain the rule and route to the decider.
3. **The policy doesn't cover it, or it's a legal or sensitive matter.** → say so and route to the Head of People. Don't improvise.

## Always route, never answer yourself

- Anything about a dispute, grievance, disciplinary matter or performance process
- Discrimination, harassment, bullying or retaliation concerns
- Protected leave and health matters (parental leave entitlements, sick leave, disability and adjustments, medical information)
- Questions about their contract's legal meaning, notice rights or termination
- Pay complaints and pay equity questions
- Immigration or right-to-work status
- Anything where the person says they're unsafe

These are legally sensitive and jurisdiction-specific. Route the same day, tell the person who will contact them and roughly when, and don't record details in shared files.

## Answer template

> {Direct answer in one sentence.}
>
> The {policy name} ({version date}) says: "{exact quote}".
>
> {What it means for their case, in one or two sentences.}
>
> {If discretion applies: This needs {role}'s approval. I've {sent it to them / you can request it by …}.}

**Example (policy answers it):**
> Yes, you can carry over up to five unused days into next year.
>
> The Leave Policy (version March 2026) says: "Employees may carry over up to five days of unused annual leave, to be taken by 31 March of the following year."
>
> So any days above five will lapse at year end, and carried-over days need to be used by 31 March.

(Illustrative wording; always quote the company's actual policy.)

**Example (discretion):**
> Working abroad for a month is possible, but it needs approval first.
>
> The Remote Work Policy (version January 2026) says: "Working from another country for more than two weeks requires approval from your manager and the Head of People."
>
> Approval can depend on tax and employment rules in that country, so the Head of People will check those. I've opened a request for you; expect a reply within {target}.

**Example (route):**
> Thanks for raising this. It's something the Head of People handles directly, so I've passed it to them in confidence, and they'll contact you by {time}. Nothing about it has been shared anywhere else.

## Before you send

- [ ] Quoted the current version (check the date).
- [ ] Didn't add conditions the policy doesn't state, or remove ones it does.
- [ ] Named who decides anything discretionary.
- [ ] Didn't include anyone else's personal information.
- [ ] Answered the question asked, not a nearby one.

## Logging and fixing gaps

Log the category (leave, expenses, benefits, conduct, working pattern, other), not the person, with time to answer. When the same gap or confusing clause appears three times, propose a policy update or an FAQ entry to the Head of People with the questions as evidence.

Useful signs a policy needs work:
- people ask the same question in different words;
- the answer requires two policies read together;
- managers give different answers;
- the policy says "appropriate" or "reasonable" where people need a number.

## Pitfalls

- Answering from memory of the policy instead of reading it again.
- Softening a rule to be helpful. If the answer is no, say so kindly and clearly.
- Promising an outcome on a discretionary decision.
- Discussing someone's question in a channel.
