# Policies and staff communication

## A policy people can use

Most school policies are long and unread. A usable one separates the rule from the procedure and tells each reader what they must do.

**Structure**

1. **Purpose** (two or three sentences): why the policy exists.
2. **Scope:** who and what it covers.
3. **Principles:** the few commitments that don't change.
4. **Rules:** numbered, short, in plain words ("Staff must…").
5. **Procedure:** step by step, by role, ideally as a flowchart or table.
6. **Roles:** who owns, approves, and monitors it.
7. **Related policies and legal basis:** links to statutory guidance and other policies.
8. **Review:** date approved, approved by, next review date, version.

**Check before approval**

- Does it reflect the current statutory guidance? (Name the edition.)
- Does it match what actually happens? A policy that says something the school doesn't do is a risk.
- Can a new staff member follow the procedure without asking?
- Does it conflict with another policy?
- Is the language plain? Read one paragraph aloud.
- Who must be consulted (staff, unions, governors, students, parents) and who must approve it?

Some policies are required by law or regulation, with set review cycles; the governance lead or the local authority can confirm the list for your school type and country.

## Policy review tracker

| Policy | Owner | Statutory? | Approved by | Last approved | Next review | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Review the tracker each term. Flag anything due within the next term and anything where the guidance has changed since approval. An incident that reveals a gap triggers a review regardless of the date.

## Weekly staff briefing

Teachers read what's short and relevant to them.

```
Staff briefing — week of [date]

Must do (by when):
- [action] — [who] — [deadline]

Need to know:
- [change, event, or reminder in one line]

Thanks:
- [specific recognition]

Coming up: [next two weeks, one line each]
Questions: [name / channel]
```

- Lead with actions. Put the deadline next to each.
- Three to six items. If it's longer, something belongs in a separate message.
- Same day and same format each week.
- Recognition named and specific ("Thanks to the Year 7 team for the parents' evening setup").

## Announcing a change

People resist changes they don't understand or had no say in. Structure the announcement:

1. **What's changing**, in one sentence.
2. **Why**: the problem it solves, with evidence if you have it.
3. **What it means for you**: by role.
4. **What stays the same.**
5. **When**: timeline, and any trial period.
6. **Support**: training, materials, who to ask.
7. **How to give feedback**, and when you'll review.

**Example opening**

> From Monday 12 January, we're moving lunchtime detentions to a central room run by the pastoral team. Teachers told us in the autumn survey that supervising their own detentions was costing them their lunch break most days. Here's what changes for you…

Where possible, test big changes with a few staff first, and announce in person before email.

## The difficult individual conversation

For a hard conversation with a staff member, prepare a one-page plan: the purpose, the facts (with dates), what you need to happen, what support you'll offer, and their likely response. If it's a formal HR matter (capability, conduct, absence), follow the school's HR policy and get HR advice first; notice and representation rules may apply.

## Common mistakes

- Policies copied from another school with the wrong name left in.
- Briefings that bury the one action that matters.
- Announcing a change by email on a Friday afternoon.
