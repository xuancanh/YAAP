# Certified and sworn translation

"Certified translation" means different things in different countries. The only definition that matters is the one used by **the authority that will receive the document**. This page is general background, not legal advice. Requirements change, so check the receiving body's current instructions for each job.

## The main types

| Type | What it usually means | Where it's common |
|---|---|---|
| Translator's certification | The translator signs a statement that the translation is complete and accurate, with their name, date and contact details. | UK and US practice for many purposes |
| Certified by a professional body member | As above, but the receiving body also asks for membership of a recognised association. | Some universities and agencies |
| Notarised translation | A notary certifies the translator's **signature** or identity, not the quality of the translation. | Some US and other purposes |
| Sworn or authorised translation | Made by a translator appointed by a court or ministry, with their seal or stamp. The translation itself has official status. | Many civil-law countries in Europe and Latin America |
| Apostille or legalisation | Certifies the origin of a public document (signature, seal) so another country accepts it. | Between countries in the 1961 Apostille Convention, or by consular legalisation otherwise |

## Two examples (check that they're current)

- **UK (GOV.UK, "Certifying a document").** For documents not in English or Welsh, the translator confirms in writing on the translation that it's "a true and accurate translation of the original document". They add the date of the translation and their full name and contact details. Individual bodies (the Home Office, courts, universities) may ask for more.
- **US immigration (USCIS).** The regulation at 8 CFR 103.2(b)(3) requires a full English translation of any foreign-language document. The translator certifies that it's complete and accurate and that they're competent to translate from that language into English. USCIS decisions have rejected partial translations and machine translations.

These are worked examples, not rules for everywhere. A Spanish court, a German registry office or a Brazilian university will each have its own rules.

## Questions to ask before quoting

1. **Who will receive it?** Name the body exactly, such as "Home Office visa application" or "Italian consulate in London".
2. **What do they ask for?** Get the link or text of their requirement. If the client doesn't have it, research the body's official site and record the URL and the date you checked.
3. **Which type is it?** Use the table above. Do they want a sworn translator in a particular country, a notary or an apostille?
4. **Original or copy?** Some bodies want the translation attached to a certified copy of the original.
5. **Format?** Paper with a wet signature, a digital signature, or a PDF?
6. **Deadline, including posting time**, if originals have to travel.

## What the desk does and doesn't do

Do:

- research and cite the requirement, with the "verify with the receiving authority" line;
- prepare the certification statement template for the translator to review and sign;
- track the job's extra steps (notary appointment, apostille office, courier);
- price the certification premium and any third-party fees as separate lines.

Don't:

- sign, certify or imply that a draft is certified. Certification is the qualified translator's act alone;
- advise whether a document will be accepted. Only the receiving body decides that;
- give a view on the client's immigration, court or legal matter. Refer them to a lawyer or adviser.

## Certified translation checklist

- [ ] Receiving body and its requirement recorded with source and date
- [ ] Complete translation: stamps, handwritten notes, seals and marginal text all translated or described in brackets, such as "[round stamp: …]"
- [ ] Names spelled as in the passport or ID the client provides, with the source of the spelling noted
- [ ] Dates and numbers checked twice against the original
- [ ] Illegible parts marked "[illegible]", never guessed
- [ ] Certification statement in the required wording, signed by the translator
- [ ] Copy of the source attached if required

## Common mistakes

- Assuming that "certified" in one country means the same somewhere else.
- Translating the main text but leaving out stamps and handwritten annotations.
- Correcting an error in the original. Translate what's there and flag it in a translator's note.
- Quoting before checking whether a sworn translator in the destination country is required.
