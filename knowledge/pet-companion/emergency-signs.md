# Emergency signs

When the owner describes any of these, the first reply is: **call your vet or the nearest emergency vet now.** Logging comes after. First aid can keep a pet stable on the way, but it doesn't replace vet care (AVMA).

Keep the owner's regular vet, the nearest 24-hour emergency vet and a poison helpline in the pet record, so the numbers are ready.

## Go now

- **Breathing trouble:** struggling to breathe, choking, blue or pale gums, nonstop coughing or gagging
- **Collapse,** can't stand, or unresponsive
- **Seizures:** the AVMA says get vet care right away for a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, seizure activity that doesn't stop, or two or more seizures in 24 hours. For a single short seizure, contact the vet afterwards.
- **Severe bleeding,** or bleeding that won't stop with firm pressure
- **Suspected poisoning,** even if the pet seems fine (signs can be delayed)
- **Swollen, hard belly with retching that brings nothing up,** especially in large, deep-chested dogs (a sign of bloat, which is life-threatening)
- **Straining to urinate and passing little or nothing,** especially in male cats
- **Heatstroke signs:** heavy panting, drooling, weakness, collapse after heat or exercise
- **Hit by a car, a fall or a fight,** even if they seem fine
- **Eye injuries,** or a sudden bulging or cloudy eye
- **Severe vomiting or diarrhoea,** especially with blood, or in very young, old or small animals
- **Signs of severe pain:** crying out, hiding and refusing to move, a hunched posture

If unsure: call the vet and describe it. Vets would rather hear about something that turns out minor.

## First-aid principles (AVMA)

- **Keep yourself safe.** Hurt or frightened animals may bite. Approach slowly; a towel or blanket helps. Never muzzle a pet that's vomiting or struggling to breathe.
- **Poisoning:** time matters. Call the vet or a poison helpline (in the US, ASPCA Animal Poison Control, 888-426-4435; a consultation fee may apply). Have the packaging or plant sample. **Don't induce vomiting or give anything unless told to by the vet or helpline.**
- **Seizures:** move objects away; don't restrain the pet or put your hand near its mouth; time the seizure. Afterwards keep them warm and quiet.
- **Bleeding:** firm pressure with a clean cloth; add layers if it soaks through rather than lifting it.
- **Moving an injured pet:** support the whole body; use a board or blanket as a stretcher for large animals.
- **Call ahead** so the clinic is ready.

## What to have ready for the call

- Species, breed, age, weight
- What happened and when
- What you've seen since (breathing, gums colour, alertness)
- For poisoning: what was eaten, how much (best guess), when, and the packaging
- Existing conditions and current medicines (from the pet record)

## After

Log what happened, what the vet said and the follow-up plan in the health log, in the vet's words.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting to see if it gets better.
- Searching online for home remedies.
- Giving human painkillers. Many are toxic to pets.
- Making the pet vomit without instruction.
