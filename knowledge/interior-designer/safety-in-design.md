# Safety in design

A beautiful room still has to be safe for the people who live in it. Run this check at concept sign-off and again on install day.

## Furniture and TV tip-over

The US Consumer Product Safety Commission's Anchor It! campaign warns that falling furniture and TVs injure and kill young children, and advises:

- anchor dressers, bookcases, tall cabinets and TVs to the wall with anti-tip kits;
- put TVs on low, sturdy bases designed for them, pushed back, or wall-mount them;
- keep heavy items on low shelves, and keep cords out of reach.

Make anchoring part of the install-day run sheet whenever children live or visit, and make sure fixings suit the wall type.

## Window covering cords

Looped and accessible cords on blinds and curtains are a strangulation risk for young children. In the US, the CPSC notes stock window coverings made under the current voluntary standard must be cordless or have inaccessible cords, and in November 2022 it approved a mandatory standard for operating cords on custom window coverings. Other countries have their own rules.

- Specify cordless or motorised options in homes with children.
- If cords are unavoidable, use cord cleats or tensioners fixed out of reach, and keep cribs, beds and climbable furniture away from windows.

## Lead paint (US homes before 1978)

US homes built before 1978 may have lead-based paint. The EPA requires paid contractors who disturb paint in those homes to be lead-safe certified and follow lead-safe work practices. Before any sanding, stripping or demolition in an older home:

- ask the age of the property;
- recommend testing where paint will be disturbed;
- use certified contractors;
- take extra care where children or pregnant people live.

Other countries have their own guidance on lead paint and asbestos in older buildings; check before specifying works.

## Glass and mirrors

- Use safety glass where building rules require it (often doors, low-level glazing, shower screens, and near stairs). Rules vary; check with the glazier.
- Fix large mirrors and heavy art with fixings rated for the weight and wall type.

## Rugs and floors

- Use rug grippers or underlays to stop slips, especially on hard floors and at stair bases.
- Avoid curled rug edges and loose runners on stairs.
- Check slip resistance for bathroom and kitchen floor finishes.

## Designing for older clients or limited mobility

- Seats at a height that's easy to get in and out of, with arms to push up from.
- Clear, wide routes and no trip-level coffee tables in main paths.
- Good, even lighting with switches at doors and by the bed.
- Contrast between floors, walls and steps for low vision.
- Lever handles instead of knobs.
- For specific needs, work with an occupational therapist; they can assess and recommend.

## Children

- Rounded corners on low tables, or corner guards.
- Secure heavy lids on toy chests and storage benches.
- No small, loose decor within reach of toddlers.
- Window restrictors on upper floors.
- Check cot and child furniture against the country's safety standards; the product should state compliance.

## Common mistakes

- Styling a nursery with corded blinds.
- Leaving tall furniture unanchored "until the client decides on placement."
- Sanding original woodwork in an old home without checking for lead.
- Heavy art on plasterboard with the wrong fixings.
