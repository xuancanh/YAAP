# Cataloguing and condition

A good record lets someone else find the item, identify it exactly, and judge its condition without seeing it.

## Item record template

```
ID: [category]-[number]       Location: [box/shelf/sleeve]
Item: [exact identity per the hobby's fields, below]
Acquired: [date] · from [source] · price paid [amount, currency]
Provenance: [previous owners, documents, certificates]
Condition: [grade in hobby vocabulary] — owner assessment / professional grade [service, cert no.]
Flaws: [each one, specific and located]
Photos: [file names]
Latest value research: [range, date] → see valuation log
Notes: [story, why it matters to the collection]
```

## Identity fields by hobby (examples)

| Hobby | Fields that pin down the exact item |
|---|---|
| Trading cards | Set, year, card number, player/character, variant or parallel, print run if numbered |
| Vinyl records | Artist, title, label, catalogue number, country, pressing, matrix/runout etchings |
| Coins | Country, denomination, year, mint mark, variety, metal |
| Stamps | Country, catalogue number (name the catalogue), perforation, watermark, used/unused |
| Comics | Title, issue number, publisher, cover date, variant cover, printing |
| Watches | Brand, model and reference number, serial, movement, year, box and papers |

Variants matter: two items that look alike can differ greatly in value. If you're unsure of the variant, record it as "unconfirmed" and what would confirm it.

## Grading vocabularies

Each hobby has its own scale. Use the hobby's words so dealers, insurers and buyers understand the record.

- **Coins:** the 1-70 numerical (Sheldon) scale is widely used, with descriptive bands such as Good, Fine, Extremely Fine, About Uncirculated and Mint State.
- **Trading cards:** major grading companies use a 1-10 scale.
- **Comics:** a 0.5-10.0 scale is standard among the main grading companies.
- **Vinyl:** a letter scale from Mint and Near Mint down through Very Good Plus, Very Good, Good and Poor, graded separately for record and sleeve.
- **Stamps:** centring and condition descriptions (for example Superb, Very Fine, Fine), plus gum and hinge state for unused stamps.
- **Watches:** no single scale; describe case, dial, crystal, movement and service history separately.

Exact definitions vary between grading companies and price guides. Note which standard you used.

## Self-grading conservatively

- Mark every self-grade as "owner assessment".
- When torn between two grades, record the lower one.
- List flaws specifically: "1 mm crease, top-left corner" rather than "light wear".
- Grade under good, even light, with magnification for small items.
- Don't clean, press or "fix" anything to improve a grade; it can reduce value and is often detectable.

Professional grading is worth considering when an item's value is high, authenticity matters for sale, or a buyer will expect it. Weigh the grading fee, shipping and insurance, and turnaround against the likely value difference.

## Photo checklist

- Front and back, square-on, even light, neutral background.
- Close-ups of every listed flaw.
- Identifying marks: serials, matrix numbers, mint marks, hallmarks, certificate numbers.
- Scale reference for size where relevant.
- For framed or cased items: in and out of the case if safe to remove.
- File names match the record ID.

## Audit

Once a year, check the catalog against the shelves: missing items, condition changes, storage problems. Log the differences with dates.
