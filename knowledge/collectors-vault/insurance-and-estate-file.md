# Insurance and estate file

The goal is that a claim or an inheritance goes smoothly. Documentation done in advance is what makes that possible.

## Insurance: what to check with the insurer

Standard home contents policies often limit cover for collections or single valuable items. Ask the insurer (and record the answer with the date):

- Is there a single-item limit? A total limit for collections?
- Above what value must items be listed individually (scheduled)?
- What proof do they need: photos, receipts, research, formal appraisal?
- Is cover replacement value or market value?
- Are accidental damage, loss away from home, and transit (shows, posting) covered?
- How often must values be updated?

Specialist collectibles insurance exists for larger collections; compare it as a factual exercise and let the user choose.

## Scheduled items list

```
Insurance schedule — prepared 2026-11-04 · policy [number] · insurer [name]

| ID | Item | Condition | Value basis | Value | Evidence | Photos |
|----|------|-----------|-------------|-------|----------|--------|
| C-014 | [exact identity] | VF (owner) | research range | 350-430 | log 2026-11-04 | C-014-1..4 |
| W-002 | [watch ref, serial] | serviced 2025 | appraisal | [amount] | appraiser report 2026-02 | W-002-1..6 |
```

Mark every value as "research" or "formal appraisal". Refresh the list yearly and when an item crosses the insurer's threshold. Keep a copy off-site.

## If something is lost, stolen or damaged

1. Safety first; for theft, report to police and get a reference number.
2. Photograph damage before moving anything (unless that causes more harm).
3. Tell the insurer promptly and follow their process.
4. Send the catalog record, photos, receipts and valuation evidence.
5. Don't throw away damaged items until the insurer says so.

## The estate note

This note helps family who don't know the hobby. It doesn't replace a will; the will is the legal instrument, and a solicitor or estate lawyer handles the formal side.

```
About my collection — last updated [date]

What it is: [one paragraph in plain words]
Where it is: [locations, keys, storage units, safe]
Where the records are: [catalog file, photos, receipts, passwords held by ___]
The notable pieces: [top items, with dated value ranges and "research, not appraisal" label]
Who to call: [trusted dealers, auction houses, clubs, appraisers, with contacts]
What not to do: [e.g. don't clean the coins; don't sell as one lot to the first offer]
Wishes: [items I'd like to go to specific people — make sure these are also in my will]
```

Encourage the user to tell the executor the note exists.

## Selling: the sale pack

For each item: the catalog record, provenance, condition photos, grading certificate if any, and the valuation trail.

Venue trade-offs to lay out:

- **Auction:** wide reach for rare items, but seller fees and a delay before payment; reserve prices protect against low results.
- **Dealer:** quick and simple, typically at a lower price than retail because the dealer needs a margin.
- **Direct sale:** potentially highest net price, but more work and more risk (payment fraud, shipping disputes).

Ask for fee schedules in writing and compare net proceeds, not headline prices. After a sale, mark the record as sold with date, venue and net amount.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming home insurance covers the collection fully.
- Values on the schedule that are years old.
- A will that says "my collection" without the estate note explaining it.
- Family selling everything as one lot because nobody knew what was valuable.
