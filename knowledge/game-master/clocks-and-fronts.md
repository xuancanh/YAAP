# Clocks and fronts

A living world means things happen when the party isn't looking. Two light tools make that manageable: **clocks** track progress toward an event, and **fronts** group a threat's clocks and moves into one page.

## Clocks

A clock is a circle split into segments (4, 6 or 8 are typical). When something advances it, you fill a segment. When it's full, the event happens.

- **4 segments:** a quick or simple threat (the guards notice the break-in).
- **6 segments:** a meaningful project (the cult finishes the ritual site).
- **8 segments:** a long campaign-scale plan (the duke seizes the throne).

Rules of thumb:

- Name the clock by its outcome, not its process: "The bridge is destroyed", not "Sabotage".
- Write what fills a segment: time passing, a failed roll, a player choice, a faction win.
- Let players see some clocks. A visible "Alarm" clock creates tension without exposition.
- Tick between sessions. In the bible, each faction gets one tick per in-game week (or whatever cadence suits) unless the party interfered.

## Fronts

A front is one threat on one page. Template:

```
Front: <name> (e.g. The Drowned Choir)
Want: <what it wants in one line>
Impending doom: <what the world looks like if it wins>
Grim portents (the clock, in order):
  1. <first visible sign>
  2. <escalation>
  3. <escalation>
  4. <doom arrives>
Cast: <key NPCs, linked to bible files>
Stakes questions: <2-3 things you want play to answer, e.g. "Will Mara betray the Choir?">
```

Stakes questions keep you from writing a plot. You don't know the answer; play decides.

## Faction turn (between sessions)

1. For each active front, ask: did the party help, hinder or ignore it?
2. Ignored: tick its clock one segment and note the visible portent.
3. Hindered: no tick, or roll back a segment, and note how the faction reacts (revenge, new route, ally sought).
4. Helped: tick two segments or give the faction a new resource.
5. Write one rumor per front the party could hear next session.

Keep it to ten minutes. If a faction hasn't mattered for three sessions, consider retiring it.

## Common mistakes

- Too many clocks. Three or four live fronts is plenty for most tables.
- Clocks that never move because the GM feels guilty. A clock that fills is what gives players' choices weight.
- Secret clocks with invisible results. If a portent happened, put it where players can stumble on it.

## Worked example

The party ignores the smugglers for two sessions to chase a dragon rumor. The "Smugglers control the docks" clock (6) goes from 2 to 4. Next session the strong start is a harbor patrol now taking bribes in the open, and a friendly fishmonger asks the party for help. The world moved; the players feel it without a lecture.
