# Encounter design checklist

The system's budget math (challenge rating, threat level, whatever it calls it) is a starting estimate. What makes encounters work is the stuff around the numbers. Use this list after the math.

## Before the session

**1. Purpose.** What is this scene for? Reveal a clue, drain resources before the boss, show the villain's reach, or just be fun. Cut fights with no purpose.

**2. Action economy.** Count actions per round on each side, not just total power. Four PCs against one big monster usually favors the PCs, because the monster acts once and can be locked down. Add minions, extra actions, or a lair effect if you want one creature to hold up.

**3. An objective beyond "kill them."** Pick one:
- protect someone or something;
- grab the item and get out;
- hold a position for a set number of rounds;
- stop the ritual before the clock fills;
- escape.

Objectives make weak enemies dangerous and give non-combat characters something to do.

**4. Terrain that matters.** At least two of: cover, elevation, a hazard (fire, a collapsing floor, a current), a chokepoint, something to throw or break.

**5. Enemy behavior.** One line per enemy group: what they want and when they flee or surrender. Most creatures don't fight to the death.

**6. Deadliness flag.** Write plainly: "Can drop a PC" or "Can kill the party if they fight to the end." Deliberate danger is fine; accidental danger is the failure.

**7. Way out.** Note at least one non-violent resolution: bribe, parley, trick, retreat.

## Non-combat encounters

- **Social:** the NPC's want, what they'll give, what they'll never give, and one thing the party could offer that changes everything.
- **Chase or exploration:** a clock for the opposition, a cost for each shortcut, and three obstacles with more than one solution.

## Live-table dials

When the math was wrong, reach for these in order:

1. **Let it stand.** A hard fight the dice made hard is drama. Ask if the table is having fun before you fix anything.
2. **Morale.** Enemies break and run when their leader falls or half their number is down.
3. **Reinforcements,** if it's too easy; arrive from a direction that changes tactics.
4. **The environment turns:** the floor gives way, the tide comes in, the city watch arrives.
5. **Hit points,** quietly, only as a last resort, and never to undo a clever player move.

## Common mistakes

- Every fight is a slugfest in an empty room.
- Treating the budget number as exact, especially at low levels or with unusual party compositions.
- No retreat option for the PCs, so a bad roll becomes a total party kill nobody wanted.

## Worked example

Prep calls for "bandits in the woods." Checklist pass: purpose is to show the bandits are paid by the baron; objective is to grab the paymaster's strongbox before he rides off (4-segment clock); terrain is a stream ford and a fallen tree; bandits flee when the paymaster does. The fight now delivers a clue, rewards fast players and ends naturally.
