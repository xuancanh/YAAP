# Etiquette by occasion

These are common norms, not rules. Families, regions and faiths differ. When the user's own circle does things differently, follow them and note it in the file.

## Weddings

- **Registry first.** If there's a list, use it; couples make them for a reason. Off-list gifts work best when they're personal and small.
- **Cash or contributions** are normal in many cultures and increasingly common everywhere. Check the invitation or ask a close friend of the couple.
- **Timing.** Sending before or soon after the wedding is common; bulky gifts go to the couple's home, not the venue.
- **Attending abroad or as a wedding party member** costs a lot already. Many couples are happy with a smaller gift; follow the user's budget.

## New babies

- Practical beats cute: nappies in the next size up, a cooked meal, help with errands.
- Clothes: buy a size or two ahead; babies outgrow newborn sizes fast.
- Ask before visiting, and keep it short.
- For siblings, a small gift for the older child is a kind touch.

## Sympathy and bereavement

- Flowers, a written card, food, or practical help are common. Some families prefer donations to a named charity; check the funeral notice.
- Words matter more than the gift. Short and sincere: "I'm so sorry. I'll always remember how she…"
- Mark the date in the occasion file for a gentle check-in later (a month on, the first anniversary). People often feel forgotten after the funeral.
- Customs differ a lot across faiths (for example, some traditions discourage flowers). When unsure, ask someone close to the family.

## Illness and hospital

- Check the ward or care home's rules before sending flowers or food; many restrict them.
- Good options: something to pass the time (books, puzzles, headphones), comfort items, practical help for the family at home.

## Work

- Keep it modest and inclusive. Group cards and pooled gifts for leaving dos and big life events are common.
- Gifts up the chain (to a boss) are often discouraged; a group card is safer.
- Some employers and public bodies have rules on accepting gifts. If the recipient is a client, supplier or official, the user should check their organisation's gifts policy.

## Hosting and visiting

- A small host gift (flowers, wine if they drink, something from home if you've travelled) is widely appreciated.
- Ask about allergies and diet before bringing food.

## Cultural notes

Some gifts carry meanings in particular cultures, such as certain numbers, colours or objects linked to bad luck or mourning. Don't rely on lists from the internet; ask the user whether the recipient's family observes any customs, and research the specific culture when the user confirms it matters.

## Thank-you notes

Prompt the user to send them after big occasions. A good note names the gift, says how it'll be used, and mentions the giver: "The pan's already had its first omelette. Thanks for remembering I'd been complaining!"

## Common mistakes

- Buying off-registry when the couple asked for cash.
- Sending flowers to a ward that doesn't allow them.
- Ignoring a "no gifts, please" request. Honor it; a card is fine.
- Forgetting the anniversary of a loss when the user asked to be reminded.

## Worked example

A colleague's father dies. The user asks what to do. Draft: a short card from the team, with a pooled donation if the notice names a charity, and a reminder in a month to check in personally. No flowers to the office.
