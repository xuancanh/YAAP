# Rules and safety checklist

Short-term rental rules vary more than almost anything else in property. What's legal in one street can be banned in the next. Work through these questions for the host's exact address, and record the answers with their source and date in the host files.

## Local rules: the questions to answer

- [ ] **Is short-term letting allowed here?** Some cities ban it, restrict it to the host's main home, or cap nights per year.
- [ ] **Registration or licence?** Is one needed, from whom, and must the number appear on the listing?
- [ ] **Planning or zoning:** does the use need permission, especially for a whole home let most of the year?
- [ ] **Building rules:** does the lease, condo/HOA rules, or freehold forbid subletting or short lets?
- [ ] **Mortgage:** does the lender allow it, or need to be told?
- [ ] **Insurance:** does the home policy cover paying guests? Many standard policies don't; a specialist policy may be needed. Platform host protection is not a substitute; read what it excludes.
- [ ] **Tax:** income tax, local occupancy or tourist taxes, and whether the platform collects any of them. This one goes to an accountant.
- [ ] **Safety law:** fire risk assessments, alarm types, gas and electrical checks.
- [ ] **Data and guest registration:** some countries require hosts to register guest identities with the authorities.

## How different places do it (examples, not rules)

These show the range. Check current details on the official pages.

- **Scotland** runs a mandatory licensing scheme covering short-term lets, from holiday cottages to rooms in a host's home. Hosts apply to their local council.
- **New York City's** registration law requires hosts to register with the Mayor's Office of Special Enforcement, and booking platforms may not process bookings for unregistered rentals.
- **England and Wales:** the Home Office guide on small paying guest accommodation explains that fire safety law applies to small B&Bs and self-catering lets, including the duty to carry out a fire risk assessment.

## Safety kit

Agencies differ by country on exact requirements; this list follows common guidance.

- [ ] **Smoke alarms.** The US Fire Administration advises alarms inside and outside each sleeping area and on every level, tested monthly, and replaced 10 years from manufacture. Test at every turnover.
- [ ] **CO alarms** wherever there's a fuel-burning appliance. The US CPSC advises alarms on each level and outside sleeping areas.
- [ ] **Fire blanket** in the kitchen and an extinguisher where the local guidance recommends one.
- [ ] **Clear escape routes** and simple fire instructions in the house manual: how to get out, where to meet, the emergency number.
- [ ] **Gas appliances serviced** and, where required, certified.
- [ ] **First-aid kit** and a torch.
- [ ] **Emergency info sheet:** address (for calling services), the host's number, nearest hospital, water stop valve, breaker box, gas shut-off.
- [ ] **Hazard check:** balcony and stair rails, pool or hot-tub access, window restrictors in upper floors, and anchored heavy furniture if children may stay.

Hidden cameras: platforms restrict them and some ban indoor cameras entirely. Disclose any outdoor devices clearly and check the platform's current policy.

## Common mistakes

- Relying on a blog post or forum for the local law.
- Assuming the platform handles all tax and licensing.
- Insurance that excludes paying guests.
- Not re-checking: these rules change often. Put a yearly review on the radar.

## When to get a professional

- A local lawyer or planning consultant for whether hosting is allowed at all.
- An accountant for tax registration and returns.
- A fire safety professional when the property is larger or more complex than the small-premises guidance covers.
