# Scenarios and runway

A scenario is useful when it changes a decision. Start from the question, change only the drivers it touches, and end with the number at which the answer flips.

## Definitions

> **Gross burn** = total cash out in the month (operating costs, capital spend, debt payments)
> **Net burn** = cash out − cash in (receipts from customers and other income)
> **Runway (months)** = cash available ÷ average net burn

- Use average net burn over the forecast months, not the last month alone, when burn is changing. Better still, run the monthly cash forecast forward and find the month cash hits the minimum the company wants to hold.
- **Lowest cash point** = the minimum month-end cash balance in the forecast period, and the month it occurs. A business can have plenty of runway on average and still run short in one month because of an annual payment or a slow collection.
- **Cash available** = bank balance, less any cash that's restricted or committed. State what you excluded.

## Scenario types

**Base, downside, upside.** For planning and fundraising. Each changes the same few drivers with stated values:

| Driver | Downside | Base | Upside | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New customers per month | | | | pipeline, conversion history |
| Retention | | | | cohort data |
| Hiring start dates | later | plan | plan | people team |
| Collection timing | slower | terms | terms | AR history |

Keep the downside realistic, not catastrophic. A downside nobody believes gets ignored. Pair it with the actions that would be taken if it happens (pause hires, cut a programme) and model those too.

**Decision scenario.** For a specific choice: "hire two engineers in Q2 or Q3", "annual vs monthly contract with a vendor", "launch in a new market". Compare option A vs option B (and "do nothing"), each from the current forecast.

## Steps

1. Write the question in one sentence and the decision-maker.
2. Copy the current forecast. Don't build a new model.
3. List every driver change per scenario, with its value and source. Change nothing else.
4. Show per scenario: revenue, cost, net burn, runway, lowest cash point and month, and the key results affected.
5. Find the trigger point (below).
6. Name the main uncertainties and what evidence would reduce them.
7. Recommend, labelled as your view.

## Finding the trigger point

The trigger is the value of a driver at which the preferred option changes. Find it by changing that driver step by step until the comparison flips, or by solving directly when the maths is simple.

**Example.** Option: hire a salesperson at a loaded cost of 8,000 per month. Each new customer brings 1,000 of gross margin per month. Ignoring ramp time, the hire pays for itself when they bring in 8 customers' worth of margin in a month. If the sales team's history says a new rep closes far fewer than that in the first quarter, the trigger is the ramp, not the cost; model ramp explicitly before recommending.

A trigger turns a debate into a check: "If pipeline coverage is above X by the end of the month, hire in Q2; if not, wait."

## Turnaround

Speed matters for decisions. To answer quickly:

- Keep the current forecast clean and up to date, so scenarios start from a trusted base.
- Keep a short list of standard scenario levers already wired into the model (hiring dates, price, growth rate, collection days).
- Send a first answer with the main drivers and caveats, then refine. Say which parts are rough.

## Presenting

One table, one chart if it helps (cash balance by month per scenario), and three to five sentences: the answer, the trigger, the biggest uncertainty, the recommendation. Detail goes in an appendix.

## Pitfalls

- Changing many drivers at once so no one knows what caused the difference.
- Showing only P&L effect for a decision that's really about cash timing.
- An upside that's just the plan with a bigger number. Tie it to a specific event.
- Presenting runway to the day. It's an estimate; round it and give the range.
