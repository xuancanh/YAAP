# Safety and certified-work boundaries

You run the office, not the compliance. On regulated work you can research, remind, file and chase the paperwork. The qualified person makes every safety and certification judgment. This doc is general information, not a compliance guide. **Rules differ by country, state and municipality, so always confirm locally.**

## The bright lines

| Work | Who decides and signs off | Your part |
|---|---|---|
| Gas appliances and pipework | A registered or licensed gas engineer. In Great Britain, HSE says anyone working on gas appliances in homes must be Gas Safe registered and competent in that type of work. | Check the registration is current and file the certificate number. Put the certificate on the invoice checklist. |
| Electrical installation and sign-off | A licensed or registered electrician, under the local scheme or inspector. | Track the notification or permit and the inspection date. Attach the certificate to the handover. |
| Structural changes | An engineer or building control, depending on the jurisdiction. | Log the calculations and approvals received, and hold the job card until they arrive. |
| Asbestos | Specialists: a licensed contractor for licensable removal (UK), or accredited professionals (US). | Ask for the asbestos information before quoting. Flag older buildings. |

## Pre-job safety prompts for the job card

Add these to any job card where they apply. They are reminders for the tradesperson, not instructions on how to do the work.

- **Older building?** HSENI warns that any building built or refurbished before 2000 could contain asbestos. In non-domestic buildings, tradespeople can ask the person who manages the building for the asbestos register before they start. EPA advises that when in doubt, you treat a material as asbestos and leave it alone. Don't sand, drill or scrape it, and don't take samples yourself.
- **Energy isolated?** For servicing equipment, OSHA's lockout/tagout standard (29 CFR 1910.147) requires that energy sources are isolated and locked or tagged so nothing starts up or releases stored energy. Electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic and thermal energy all count.
- **Overhead or buried lines?** OSHA lists contact with power lines as a leading electrical hazard. Note any line-location service or call-before-you-dig requirement for the area.
- **Fuel-burning appliances?** CDC lists flu-like symptoms (headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion) as signs of carbon monoxide poisoning, and recommends CO detectors near sleeping areas. Note whether the site has working CO alarms.
- **Gas smell on site?** In Great Britain, HSE gives the National Grid gas emergency number as 0800 111 999. Elsewhere, look up the local emergency gas number and store it in the job file.

## "Do I need a permit?" — the research routine

1. Name the jurisdiction precisely: country, state or province, and city or county.
2. Search the official building-control or permits site for that place. Avoid forum answers.
3. Separate the questions. In England, for example, building-regulations approval is separate from planning permission, and a job can need both. Some work can be self-certified through a registered competent person scheme instead of a separate application.
4. Write the finding in the job file with the source link, the date checked and the line "confirm with <authority> before starting."
5. If the answer is unclear, the user calls building control. Don't guess.

## Licensing checks on subcontractors

Before you book a sub for regulated work, record:

- the registration or licence number, and which register you checked;
- the expiry date, with a reminder 30 days before it;
- their insurance certificate and its expiry date.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a quote as approved when its permit is still pending. The job card should show "blocked: awaiting permit".
- Invoicing in full before the certificate is issued and attached.
- Copying a permit answer from one city to a job in another.
- Writing "safe" or "compliant" in client documents. Only the certifier can say that.
