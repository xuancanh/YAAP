# Verification toolkit

Every checkable claim gets a source or gets cut. These are the methods, by claim type.

## Five questions for any claim

1. **Who** says so, and how do they know? (Witness, document, analysis, hearsay.)
2. **What's the original?** Go to the primary source, not the article or press release about it.
3. **When?** Is it current, or an old fact being recirculated?
4. **What would prove it wrong?** Look for that.
5. **Who else can confirm it independently?** Two sources who heard it from the same person are one source.

## Numbers

- Recompute from the original table or dataset. Check units, time periods and base (per cent of what?).
- Watch for percentage vs percentage points, and averages vs medians.
- Check the definition: "unemployment" or "crime" can be measured several ways.
- If a number seems surprising, it might be wrong. Ask the source how it was calculated.

## Quotes

- Verify verbatim against the recording or notes, with timestamp.
- Check context: does the surrounding conversation change the meaning?
- Follow the outlet's rules on cleaning up quotes (removing "um", fixing grammar). Flag any edit that shifts meaning.
- For quotes from documents or speeches, find the original text or video.

## Names, titles and facts about people

- Spelling: check against an official source (company site, government directory, the person's own profile), and confirm with the person if possible.
- Title and employer: people move jobs mid-story; re-check close to publication.
- Ages and dates: from records, not memory.

## Documents

- Where did it come from, and through how many hands?
- Does it look right: letterhead, formatting, signatures, dates, reference numbers compared with known genuine examples?
- Check file metadata where available (creation dates, author), knowing it can be edited.
- Seek confirmation from someone who'd know (the issuing body, a recipient), without exposing your source.

## Images and video

- **Reverse image search** (several engines) to find earlier copies. Old images often resurface as new events.
- **Location:** match landmarks, signs, terrain, shadows with maps and street imagery.
- **Time:** weather records, shadows, events visible in the frame.
- **Uploader:** who posted first? Contact them; ask for the original file.
- **Manipulation:** inconsistent lighting, warped edges, AI artefacts (hands, text). Absence of signs isn't proof of authenticity.

## Social accounts

- Account age, posting history and network. A new account with few followers posting "eyewitness" footage needs more checking.
- Is it the real person or organisation? Look for links from their official site.
- Archive posts (and note the URL and time) before they're deleted.

## Fact-check log entry

```
Claim: [text as in draft, para #]
Type: number / quote / name / event / characterisation
Source: [document p., recording 14:32, dataset]
Checked by / date:
Status: confirmed / corrected / cut / needs legal read
Note:
```

## Common mistakes

- Treating a press release's summary of a report as the report.
- One source that's really two retellings of the same source.
- Checking the quote but not the context.
- Assuming viral footage is from the event it's captioned with.

## Worked example

A draft says "complaints rose 40% last year." The fact-check traces it to a council press release; the underlying report shows a 40-point rise in a satisfaction index, not complaints, and complaints actually rose by a smaller figure. The claim is corrected to the report's figure and wording, with the page cited in the log.
