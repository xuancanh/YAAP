# Attribution and recording

Misunderstood ground rules can burn a source or a reporter's reputation. Agree them in advance, in plain words, and write them down.

## Common definitions

These terms don't mean the same thing to everyone. Always check what the source thinks they mean, and follow the outlet's policy.

| Term | Common meaning |
|---|---|
| **On the record** | Everything can be used and attributed to the person by name and title. |
| **On background** (not for attribution) | Information can be used and attributed to a description agreed with the source ("a senior official at the agency"), not by name. |
| **Deep background** | Information can be used but not attributed at all; the reporter must state it in their own voice or confirm elsewhere. |
| **Off the record** | Information can't be used. Some reporters use it only as a lead to confirm elsewhere; others not at all. Agree which. |

## Agreeing terms

1. **Before the interview,** not after a revealing answer. Default to on the record unless agreed otherwise.
2. **Say it back:** "So I can quote you by name on the budget, and the staffing part is background, attributed to 'a person familiar with the plans.' Right?"
3. **Agree the description** for background sources precisely.
4. **Retroactive requests** ("that was off the record") aren't binding by convention, but outlets handle them differently. Escalate to the editor.
5. **Write it down** in the interview file with date and time.
6. **Promises of confidentiality** are serious; some outlets require an editor's approval. Know your outlet's rules before offering.

## Anonymous sources

Use them when there's no other way to get important information and the source faces real risk if named. Common practice:

- the editor knows who the source is;
- explain to readers why anonymity was granted;
- describe the source's position and how they know;
- corroborate the information elsewhere.

## Recording consent

Recording laws differ by country and, in the US, by state. This is general information; check the jurisdiction and get legal advice when unsure.

- **US:** the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press explains that federal law and most states allow recording with the consent of one party to the conversation (which can be the reporter), while a minority of states require the consent of all parties. Calls between states with different rules are a grey area; the safe practice is to get everyone's consent.
- **Other countries** have their own rules, and data protection law may apply to how recordings are stored and used.
- **Best practice everywhere:** say you're recording at the start and get consent on the recording itself. "I'm recording this so I quote you accurately. Is that okay?"

## Storing recordings and notes

- Keep them securely, in line with the outlet's policy, for as long as the outlet requires (they may matter in a legal dispute).
- Label files by codename for confidential sources.
- Don't upload recordings with confidential sources to third-party transcription services without considering who can access them.

## Common mistakes

- Agreeing "off the record" without defining it.
- Background descriptions so specific they identify the source.
- Recording without telling the person, in a place that needs all-party consent.
- Losing track of which parts of a long interview were on which terms.

## Worked example

A council employee will talk about a contract only if not named. The reporter agrees background, attributed to "a council staff member with knowledge of the contract," confirms that wording back, logs it, tells the editor the source's identity, and corroborates the key claim with a document obtained separately before publication.
