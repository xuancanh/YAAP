# Legal risk red flags

This is general education, not legal advice. Defamation and privacy law differ significantly by country (and in the US, by state). The desk's job is to spot risk and flag it; a lawyer decides.

## Defamation in plain terms

Defamation is publishing a false statement of fact about an identifiable person or organisation that harms their reputation. Details vary, but most legal systems look at:

- **Is it a statement of fact** (provable true or false) or opinion? Opinion dressed as fact ("the mayor is a thief") is still risky.
- **Is it about an identifiable person?** Not naming someone doesn't help if readers can work out who.
- **Is it false?** Truth is generally a defence, but in many places the publisher must be able to prove it.
- **Does it harm reputation?** In England and Wales, the Defamation Act 2013 requires the statement to have caused, or be likely to cause, serious harm.
- **Fault:** in the US, public officials and public figures must generally show the publisher knew the statement was false or acted with reckless disregard for the truth; private individuals usually face a lower bar. Other countries take different approaches.

Repeating someone else's defamatory claim ("X said that Y…") can still be defamation by the publisher in many jurisdictions.

## Red flags: get a legal read

Flag the draft for the editor and lawyer when it:

- [ ] accuses a named or identifiable person of a crime, dishonesty, sexual misconduct or professional incompetence;
- [ ] relies on a single or anonymous source for a damaging claim;
- [ ] characterises conduct ("cover-up", "fraud", "abuse") beyond what the evidence shows;
- [ ] reports on ongoing criminal proceedings (contempt and reporting restrictions apply in many countries);
- [ ] involves children, victims of sexual offences, or health information;
- [ ] uses leaked documents, recordings or hacked data;
- [ ] publishes private information (address, medical, family) without a clear public interest;
- [ ] has a subject who has threatened legal action.

## Right of reply

Giving the subject a fair chance to respond is good journalism and reduces legal risk in many jurisdictions.

The right-of-reply letter should:

1. state each allegation specifically, with dates and facts;
2. ask for a response to each;
3. give a reasonable deadline for the complexity (not "by 5pm today" for a complex investigation);
4. say how the response will be used.

Log when it was sent, any reply, and how it's reflected in the story. Report their response fairly, even if it's "declined to comment."

## Privacy

Even true information can create legal or ethical risk if it's private and there's no public interest. The SPJ Code's "minimise harm" principle is a good test: does the public need this, or is it just interesting? Be especially careful with children, grief, health and sexual orientation.

## Corrections

When something is wrong, correct it promptly and clearly, and log it in the corrections file. A fast, honest correction often reduces both harm and legal exposure. Don't remove an article or alter it silently without talking to the editor and lawyer.

## Common mistakes

- "Allegedly" as a magic shield. It doesn't fix an unsupported claim.
- Headlines and social posts that go further than the story.
- Right-of-reply deadlines that aren't really a chance to reply.
- Publishing first and checking later because a competitor might.

## Worked example

A draft says a contractor "defrauded" the city. The evidence shows invoices for work not yet done and an audit querying them. The desk flags it: "defrauded" asserts a crime the audit doesn't establish. Suggested wording sticks to the documents ("billed for work an audit says had not been completed"), and the right-of-reply letter lists each invoice. The draft goes to the lawyer before publication.
