# Giving feedback

Useful feedback is specific, about the work, and pitched at the level the maker asked for. Praise alone doesn't help them improve; harshness makes them stop sharing.

## 1. Agree the level first

Ask, if they haven't said: "What kind of feedback would help most right now?"

| Level | Looks at | When it's useful |
|---|---|---|
| Big picture | Does it work overall? Stakes, structure, pacing, promise to the audience | Early drafts, first cuts |
| Section / scene | Does this part do its job? Whose scene is it, does it start late and end early | Middle drafts |
| Line / detail | Word choice, rhythm, dialogue, a phrase in a melody, a line in a drawing | Near-final work |

Line edits on a first draft are mostly wasted; that paragraph may be cut. Match the level.

## 2. The shape of a critique

1. **What's working, quoted.** Name the strongest line, image or moment, and say why it works. Makers revise better when they know what to protect.
2. **The two or three most important issues.** Not every issue. Rank them.
3. **For each issue:** where it happens, what effect it has on you as a reader, viewer or listener, and one or two possible directions.
4. **A question or two** that might open something up.

Describe your experience ("I lost track of who was speaking here") rather than ruling ("This is confusing"). It's harder to argue with and easier to act on.

## 3. Suggest, don't take over

- Mark and comment; don't rewrite whole passages.
- Offer options: "You could cut this paragraph, or move it before the reveal."
- If you show an example rewrite, keep it short and label it as one possibility.

## Craft prompts by medium

- **Fiction:** What does the character want in this scene? What's in the way? What changes by the end?
- **Poetry:** Which line carries the most weight? Is any word doing nothing?
- **Music:** Where does the energy rise and fall? Is there contrast between sections?
- **Drawing/painting:** Where does the eye go first? Is that where the maker wants it?
- **Photography:** What's the subject? Is anything at the edges pulling attention?

## Worked example

The user shares a short story opening and asks, "Is this any good?"

Ask the level: they say big picture. Then:

> "The line 'the kettle had been screaming for a while before anyone noticed' is a great start: it tells me this family isn't paying attention to each other before anything happens.
>
> Two things to look at. First, the next page is backstory about the grandmother. I wanted to stay in the kitchen with the kettle; the backstory might land harder later, once I care. Second, I wasn't sure whose story this is: the daughter or the mother. Picking one point of view for the opening could make it sharper.
>
> Question: what does the daughter want when she walks in?"

## Common mistakes

- "I loved it!" with nothing specific.
- Twenty notes of equal weight.
- Feedback on what you'd have made instead of what they're making.
- Line editing when they asked about structure.
- Commenting on the person ("you always…") instead of the work.

## Receiving feedback (to share with the user)

Listen fully, ask clarifying questions, thank the reader, and decide later. Notes that several readers give independently are usually worth acting on; a single reader's taste may not be.
