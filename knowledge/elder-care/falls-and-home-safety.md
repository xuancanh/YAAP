# Falls and home safety checklist

Falls are a leading cause of injury in older adults, and most happen at home. Public-health guidance such as CDC's STEADI programme (checked 2026-11) groups prevention into four moves: talk to the health provider about fall risk, review medicines, get eyes and feet checked, and make the home safer. Use this checklist for the last one, and prompt the other three.

## After a fall or near-miss

1. **Injury, head strike, can't get up, or new confusion?** Emergency services or urgent medical care first.
2. Tell the primary care provider about every fall, even without injury. A fall is a reason for a fall-risk check.
3. Log it: date, time, place, what they were doing, footwear, lighting, any dizziness beforehand, medicines changed recently.
4. Ask whether a physiotherapist or occupational therapist home assessment is available locally.
5. Ask about a personal alarm or a plan for getting help if they fall when alone.

## Room-by-room checklist

**Floors and stairs**
- [ ] Clear walkways: no cords, clutter or low furniture in paths.
- [ ] Remove loose rugs, or secure them firmly.
- [ ] Sturdy handrails on both sides of stairs; good lighting with switches at top and bottom.
- [ ] Step edges visible (contrasting strip if hard to see).

**Bathroom**
- [ ] Grab bars by the toilet and in the shower or bath (installed into studs, not suction cups).
- [ ] Non-slip mat or strips in the bath or shower.
- [ ] Shower chair or raised toilet seat if standing or rising is hard.

**Bedroom**
- [ ] A lamp reachable from bed; night lights on the route to the bathroom.
- [ ] Bed at a height where feet rest flat when sitting.
- [ ] Phone reachable from bed.

**Kitchen**
- [ ] Everyday items between waist and shoulder height; no step stools.
- [ ] Spills wiped straight away.

**Outside and entrances**
- [ ] Steps even, lit, with rails; paths clear of leaves, ice and moss.

**The person**
- [ ] Shoes that fit, with thin non-slip soles and a closed heel, worn indoors too.
- [ ] Glasses current and clean; hearing aids in.
- [ ] Mobility aid fitted by a professional and actually used.
- [ ] Not rushing to the bathroom or the phone (a cordless or mobile phone helps).

## Strength and balance

Exercise programmes that build strength and balance reduce fall risk. Ask the provider or a physiotherapist which programme suits the person; do not prescribe exercises for someone with known health conditions.

## Common mistakes

- Not telling the doctor about a fall "because nothing broke".
- Buying equipment without an assessment, so it is the wrong height or never used.
- Restricting activity out of fear, which weakens muscles and raises risk.
- Fixing the home and forgetting the medicine review.
