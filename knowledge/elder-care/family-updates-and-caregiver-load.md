# Family updates and caregiver load

Most family fractures in elder care come from three things: people hearing news late or second-hand, decisions nobody wrote down, and one person quietly carrying most of the load. Regular, factual updates fix the first two; making work visible fixes the third.

## Weekly update template

```
Update on Mum — week of <date>
How she is: <two sentences, factual>
Appointments this week: <what happened, what changed>
Coming up: <dates, who is taking her>
Decisions needed: <the question, the options, by when>
Help needed: <specific tasks with dates — "Someone to cover Thursday 2–5pm">
```

Keep it the same shape every week so it is easy to skim. Send to everyone at once; side-channel updates breed suspicion.

## Decision record

For any significant decision (moving, new care, money, medical choices made with clinicians):

```
Decision: <what>          Date: <date>
Who was involved, including the person themselves:
Options considered:
What the person wants (their words):
Advice received (from whom):
Agreed: <what, who does it, by when>
Review date:
```

The person being cared for stays at the centre. Their wishes are recorded first, and decisions that fall under legal authority (such as a power of attorney) follow that document and local law.

## Making the load visible

List the recurring tasks with rough hours per week: appointments, shopping, medicines, paperwork, visits, night calls. Put a name next to each. Seeing it on paper usually does more than arguing about fairness. Siblings far away can take tasks that work remotely: calling the pharmacy, bills, research, booking.

## Signs of caregiver burnout

Name these gently and regularly, not only in a crisis:

- constant tiredness, poor sleep, getting ill more often;
- irritability, resentment or feeling trapped;
- withdrawing from friends and activities;
- neglecting their own appointments and health;
- feeling hopeless, or thoughts of harming themselves or the person.

The last one is urgent: encourage them to contact their doctor or a crisis line in their country today.

## Finding respite (country-specific)

Respite means short-term care so the regular caregiver can rest: a sitter for an afternoon, adult day programmes, or a short stay in a care home. Where to look depends on the country:

- **United States:** the Eldercare Locator (ACL) and ARCH's National Respite Locator list local services.
- **United Kingdom:** a carer's assessment from the local council, and Carers UK's advice pages.
- **Elsewhere:** the local health or social-services authority, and national caregiver charities.

Check eligibility and costs with the provider; these change.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for a crisis before asking for help.
- Updates that are all feelings and no facts, or all facts and no asks.
- Decisions made by whoever is in the room, then relitigated.
