# Medication schedule safety

The job here is fidelity: the regimen as prescribed, on time, with refills that never run out. Clinical judgment is never part of it. Any question about whether, how much, or what interacts goes to the pharmacist or prescriber.

## The master medication list

Keep one list, kept current, and bring it to every appointment. The U.S. FDA's advice for older adults and caregivers includes keeping such a list and reviewing it with the pharmacist (FDA consumer update, checked 2026-11). For each item record:

- name (brand and generic) and strength, copied from the label;
- what it is for, in the prescriber's words;
- when and how it is taken, exactly as on the label (with food, at bedtime);
- prescriber, pharmacy, start date;
- refill date, repeats left, and supply on hand;
- over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, herbal products and supplements too.

Copy from labels, never from memory. If a label and the list disagree, the label is checked with the pharmacist, not "fixed" by guessing.

## The schedule

Turn the list into time slots (morning, midday, evening, bedtime) with checkboxes. Note special instructions beside the slot. A pill organiser filled weekly, from the labelled boxes, makes misses visible.

## Refill radar

- Track days of supply remaining per medicine.
- Request refills when about a week or two of supply is left, earlier around holidays or if prescriptions need renewing by the doctor.
- Using one pharmacy where possible lets the pharmacist see the whole picture.

## Always route to a professional

Send these to the pharmacist or prescriber, every time, without offering an opinion:

- "Can they skip / double up / split / crush this?"
- "Is it OK with this new over-the-counter or supplement?"
- New symptoms after a change: dizziness, drowsiness, confusion, falls, stomach upset, rash. Medicine side effects in older adults can look like other problems; CDC's STEADI programme includes medication review as a fall-prevention step.
- Pills that look different from last time, or unlabelled pills. Never identify a pill by guesswork.
- A missed dose: the pharmacist or the leaflet says what to do, not the assistant.

Call emergency services for signs of a serious reaction or overdose, such as trouble breathing, swelling of face or throat, collapse, or sudden severe confusion. Poison control lines (where they exist in the person's country) also advise on accidental overdose.

## Review moments

Ask for a full medication review with the pharmacist or doctor:

- after every hospital stay (discharge lists often change medicines);
- when a new prescriber joins;
- at least yearly, or when the list keeps growing.

## Common mistakes

- Two lists (one at home, one in the bag) that drift apart.
- Supplements and creams left off the list.
- Old, stopped medicines still in the house and ending up in the organiser. Ask the pharmacy about safe disposal.
- Answering "can she take this?" because the answer seems obvious.
