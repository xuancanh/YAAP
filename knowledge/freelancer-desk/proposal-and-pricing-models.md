# Proposal template and pricing models

A proposal should prove you understood the problem, then make saying yes easy. Keep it short; most clients read the summary and the price.

## Proposal template

```
<Client> — <Project name>          <date>, valid until <date>

1. What you told us
   Their goal, in their words. The problem behind the request. Constraints (date, budget, people).

2. What we'll do
   Approach in 3–5 steps. What they'll get at each step.

3. Deliverables
   Concrete list: "Homepage design, two rounds of revisions", not "website work".

4. Not included
   The things they might assume are in. (This line prevents most scope fights.)

5. Timeline
   Start date, milestones, end date; what you need from them and by when.

6. Investment
   Option A / B / C, or a single price; payment schedule.

7. Next step
   "Reply to accept and I'll send the agreement and first invoice."
```

## Choosing a pricing model

| Model | Fits when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Hourly** | Scope is genuinely unknown; ongoing small tasks | Punishes efficiency; client anxiety about the meter. Add a cap or estimate range |
| **Day rate** | Workshops, on-site work, embedded sprints | Define what a day is (hours, travel) |
| **Fixed price** | Clear deliverables and scope | Needs tight "not included" and a change process |
| **Value-based** | You can link the work to a measurable result for the client | Requires a frank conversation about the value; not for every client |
| **Retainer** | Ongoing access or a set monthly volume | Define what's included, rollover rules, and response times |

## Pricing a fixed-price job

1. Break the work into tasks; estimate hours for each, as a range.
2. Use the high end for anything new or unclear.
3. Add time for meetings, revisions, admin and project management (often underestimated).
4. Multiply by your effective hourly rate (see [rate math](rate-math.md)).
5. Sense-check against the value to the client and the market.

## Three options

Offering a small, medium and large option lets the client choose how much, not whether. Make the middle the one you recommend, with the differences clear (scope, speed, support).

## Worked example

Inquiry: "We need a new brochure." Discovery call shows the real goal is getting trade-show leads in March. Proposal: Option A brochure only; Option B brochure plus a matching one-page landing site; Option C adds a follow-up email sequence. "Not included: printing, photography." Payment 50% to start, 50% on delivery. Valid for 30 days.

## Common mistakes

- Pricing before understanding the goal.
- No "not included" section.
- Estimates without revision rounds or meeting time.
- Proposals that read like a CV instead of being about the client.
- Leaving out the expiry date, then being held to an old price.
