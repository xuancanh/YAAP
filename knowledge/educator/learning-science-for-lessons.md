# Learning science for lesson design

A handful of principles have strong, repeated support in research and translate directly into planning choices. Use them as defaults; adjust for age and subject.

## The core principles

**Retrieval practice.** Pulling information out of memory strengthens it more than re-reading it. Low-stakes quizzes, "brain dumps", and answering from memory before checking notes all count. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate practice testing as one of the two highest-utility techniques they reviewed.

**Spacing.** Practice spread over days and weeks beats the same amount crammed into one go. It is the other high-utility technique in the same review. In planning terms: revisit last week's and last month's content in starters, not just today's.

**Interleaving.** Mixing problem types (rather than twenty of the same kind in a row) helps students learn *which* method a problem needs. Use it once the basics of each type are secure; it feels harder, and that is the point.

**Worked examples, then fading.** Novices learn faster from studying a fully worked example than from solving cold. Then remove steps progressively until students solve independently ("I do, we do, you do").

**Cognitive load.** Working memory is small. Cut anything on the slide or sheet that is not needed, put labels next to the thing they label, and teach one new idea at a time before combining.

**Feedback that moves the task forward.** Feedback works best when it is specific, about the task (not the person), and acted on soon. The EEF Toolkit lists feedback among its higher-impact, low-cost approaches.

## Building them into a lesson

- **Starter (5 min):** three to five retrieval questions: one from last lesson, one from last week, one from earlier in the term.
- **New content:** one worked example per new step, then a near-identical "your turn" problem.
- **Practice:** move from blocked (same type) to mixed once accuracy is high.
- **Check:** a hinge question before moving on (see [assessment design](assessment-design.md)).
- **Exit:** two questions that tell you who needs what tomorrow.

## Common mistakes

- Treating highlighting and re-reading as revision. They feel productive and do little on their own.
- Retrieval quizzes that are graded and high-stakes. Keep them low-stakes so students attempt from memory.
- Homework on today's topic only. Add a few spaced questions from older topics.
- Interleaving too early, before students can do each type alone.
- Matching teaching to "learning styles" (visual, auditory, kinaesthetic). It lacks good evidence; vary modes because the content needs it, not to match a style.

## Quick check for any plan

1. Where do students retrieve something from memory?
2. Where does older content come back?
3. Is there a worked example before independent work?
4. What on the materials could be cut?
5. How will the teacher know, before the end, who has it?
