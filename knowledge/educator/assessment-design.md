# Assessment design: questions, distractors, rubrics

Every check should tell the teacher something they will act on. If every answer would lead to the same next step, the question is not doing its job.

## Multiple-choice questions that diagnose

1. **Start from the misconception.** Each wrong option should be the answer a student with a specific misunderstanding would choose. Note which misconception each one maps to.
2. **One clearly right answer.** Would an expert hesitate? Then rewrite.
3. **Similar length and form.** The longest, most careful option is often the right one, and students learn that.
4. **Avoid "all of the above" and "none of the above".** They test test-taking more than content.
5. **The stem holds the question.** A student should be able to answer before seeing the options.

**Example.** *A plant is put in a dark cupboard for a week. Why does it lose mass?*

- A. It uses stored food for respiration and cannot make more by photosynthesis. (correct)
- B. Plants get their food from the soil, and the soil dried out. (misconception: food comes from soil)
- C. Plants only respire in the dark. (misconception: respiration happens only at night)
- D. Light is what plants are made of. (misconception: light as a substance)

## Hinge questions

A single question at the pivot point of a lesson, answerable in under a minute by everyone at once (fingers, cards, whiteboards). Decide in advance: if most are right, move on; if not, re-teach a different way. Keep it to one concept.

## Exit tickets

Two or three questions, one of which targets the most likely misconception. Sort results into piles that map to tomorrow's groups, not into grades.

## Rubrics students can use

- **Criteria in student language.** "I explain *why*, not just *what*" beats "Demonstrates analytical depth".
- **Levels describe real differences in the work**, not just intensity words. Avoid ladders like *limited / some / good / excellent* without saying what changes.
- **Three or four levels** are usually enough to be distinguishable.
- **Separate criteria that vary independently** (content accuracy vs. organisation), and do not double-count.
- **Pair with an exemplar** at each level where possible; students learn more from seeing the difference.

Rubric row example (persuasive paragraph, "Evidence"):

| Beginning | Developing | Secure | Extending |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gives an opinion with no reason | Gives a reason but no example or fact | Gives a reason backed by a specific example or fact | Uses two pieces of evidence and explains how each supports the point |

## Self-assessment

Turn each rubric row into an "I can" checklist students tick before handing in, and ask them to underline the evidence in their own work. It makes the criteria concrete and cuts marking time.

## Common mistakes

- Distractors that are obviously silly, so the question tests nothing.
- Quizzes that only test recall when the objective was application.
- Rubrics written after marking, to justify grades.
- Grading every formative check, which turns retrieval practice into high-stakes testing.
- Marking decisions handed to the assistant. The teacher marks; the assistant drafts criteria and checks consistency.
