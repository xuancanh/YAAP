# Parent message templates

Families become allies when the first message they hear from school is not a problem. Aim for a positive contact for every student early in the year, and keep concern messages factual and solution-focused.

## Ground rules

- Check the school's policy on channels (email, app, phone) and what must go through a leader. Follow it.
- Short sentences, no jargon or acronyms. Offer translation where the school provides it.
- One message, one purpose.
- Facts, not labels: "did not hand in the last three homework tasks", not "lazy" or "disengaged".
- Never include other students' names or details.
- Anything involving safeguarding, special-education processes or discipline outcomes follows school procedure, not a template. Flag it to the teacher.

## Positive contact

```
Subject: A good moment from <Student> today

Hi <Parent name>,
I wanted to share something good: today <Student> <specific thing they did> in <subject>.
<One sentence on why it matters.>
Thanks for your support at home.
<Teacher>
```

Specific beats generic. "Explained to the group why the experiment needed a control" is worth far more than "worked hard".

## Concern message

```
Subject: Checking in about <Student>'s <area>

Hi <Parent name>,
I'm getting in touch because <specific, observable fact with dates>.
In class I've tried <what you've done>. <Student> also does well at <genuine strength>.
I'd like to work on this together. Could we <talk by phone / meet> this week?
Some times that work for me: <options>.
<Teacher>
```

Order matters: the fact, what you've tried, a genuine strength, an invitation. Keep the long history out of writing; save it for the conversation.

## Newsletter item

- A headline that says what happened or what is coming.
- Two or three sentences.
- The one thing families can do (a question to ask at dinner, a date to note).
- Dates in one consistent format, with the day of the week.

Example: *"Year 5 starts fractions next week. Ask your child to share a pizza into equal parts and name them. Quiz on Friday 14 November."*

## Responding to an upset parent

1. Acknowledge the feeling and thank them for raising it.
2. Restate what you understand the concern to be.
3. Give the facts you know; do not speculate.
4. Offer a conversation and a next step with a date.
5. Loop in a leader if the school's policy says so or the tone escalates.

## Common mistakes

- The first-ever contact is a complaint.
- Sending while annoyed. Draft, wait, reread.
- Long emails that bury the ask.
- Promising outcomes (grades, placements) that are not the teacher's call.
