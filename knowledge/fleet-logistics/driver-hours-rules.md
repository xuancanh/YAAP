# Driver-hours rules: what to check

**Country- and time-sensitive.** Driver-hours rules are law. Which set applies depends on the country, the vehicle's weight and type, whether it carries goods or passengers, the distance, and the kind of work. Small vans often fall under different rules from heavy trucks, or only under general working-time law. Always identify the applicable rules from the regulator's site, record the source and date checked, and have the operator confirm.

## The kinds of limits to look for

Most regimes combine some of these:

- **Daily driving limit.**
- **Maximum driving before a break,** and the minimum break length.
- **Daily rest** between working days.
- **Weekly (and sometimes fortnightly) driving limits.**
- **Weekly rest.**
- **A "duty window"**: total time from starting work to finishing, including non-driving work.
- **Working-time limits** that include loading, paperwork and waiting, not just driving.
- **Record-keeping:** tachographs, electronic logging devices, or written records, and how long to keep them.
- **Exemptions:** short-distance, certain trades, or vehicles below a weight limit.

## Reference example 1: GB "assimilated rules" (formerly EU rules)

For vehicles over 3.5 tonnes in GB or travelling to, from or through the EU, GOV.UK lists (checked 2026-11):

- daily driving 9 hours, extendable to 10 hours twice a week;
- weekly driving 56 hours; 90 hours in any two consecutive weeks;
- after no more than 4.5 hours of driving, breaks totalling at least 45 minutes;
- daily rest at least 11 hours, reducible to 9 hours up to three times between weekly rests;
- weekly rest of 45 hours, reducible to 24 hours every other week, with conditions.

Separate **GB domestic rules** apply to many vehicles exempt from these; mixing the two in one week has its own rules (see GOV.UK's page on driving under different rules).

## Reference example 2: US federal hours of service

For property-carrying commercial vehicles in interstate commerce, the FMCSA summary includes (checked 2026-11; confirm against 49 CFR Part 395):

- up to 11 hours of driving after 10 consecutive hours off duty;
- no driving beyond the 14th hour after coming on duty;
- a 30-minute break after 8 cumulative hours of driving;
- a 60-hour / 7-day or 70-hour / 8-day on-duty limit.

There are exceptions (short-haul, adverse conditions) with specific conditions. States set their own rules for intrastate work.

These two examples show the shape of the rules. They are not a substitute for checking the rules that apply to this fleet.

## Rostering checklist

- [ ] Which rule set applies to each vehicle and type of run? Recorded with source and date.
- [ ] Each driver's hours from the past week or fortnight, including work for other employers.
- [ ] Planned runs fit daily and weekly driving limits **with a buffer** for traffic and delays.
- [ ] Breaks planned at realistic stopping points.
- [ ] Daily and weekly rest respected between shifts, including early starts after late finishes.
- [ ] Records (tachograph, logs) downloaded and kept as required.
- [ ] Cover planned for sickness and holidays without pushing others over limits.
- [ ] Fairness: unpopular early, late and long runs rotated.

## When a run won't fit

Don't schedule it. Options: split the run, add a second driver, move it to tomorrow, or subcontract. Tell the customer early.

## Common mistakes

- Planning to the legal maximum with no buffer.
- Forgetting non-driving work (loading, admin) that counts towards working time.
- Assuming vans are outside all rules.
- Relying on a summary (including this one) instead of the regulator's text.
