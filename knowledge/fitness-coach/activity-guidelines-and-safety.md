# Activity guidelines and safety

Public-health guidelines give a sensible baseline for most adults. Training plans can go beyond them; they're a floor for health, not a ceiling for performance.

## The baseline (adults)

From the WHO 2020 guidelines and CDC's adult overview (checked 2026-11):

- **Aerobic activity:** 150–300 minutes a week of moderate intensity, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous intensity, or an equivalent mix (WHO). CDC states at least 150 minutes moderate or 75 vigorous, and notes more brings extra benefit.
- **Muscle strengthening:** at moderate or greater intensity, working all major muscle groups (legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders, arms), on **two or more days a week**.
- **Sit less:** replacing sitting with any activity, even light, has benefits. Some activity is better than none.
- **Spread it out:** the minutes can be split across the week, for example 30 minutes on five days.

**Older adults (65+)** follow the same targets where able, and WHO adds varied multicomponent activity that emphasises balance and strength on three or more days a week, to help prevent falls.

Separate WHO recommendations exist for pregnancy and postpartum, and for people with chronic conditions or disability. Point people to their health provider for advice that fits them.

## Judging intensity

- **Talk test:** at moderate intensity you can talk but not sing; at vigorous intensity you can say only a few words before pausing for breath.
- **Effort scale (0–10):** moderate is roughly a 5–6, vigorous 7–8, for that person. It adjusts automatically to fitness level.

## Stop and seek help

Stop exercising and get medical help (emergency services if severe or not settling) for:

- chest pain, pressure or tightness;
- fainting, near-fainting or severe dizziness;
- unusual shortness of breath for the effort;
- a racing or irregular heartbeat that doesn't settle;
- sudden weakness, numbness or difficulty speaking.

## See a professional before pushing on

- Pain that is sharp, in a joint, worsens during exercise, or changes how they move.
- Pain or swelling that lasts more than a few days after a session.
- Any injury that hasn't been assessed.
- Pregnancy or postpartum, unless already cleared.
- Known heart, lung, kidney or metabolic conditions, or a doctor's past advice to limit activity.
- Starting vigorous training after being inactive, especially with health conditions or at an older age.

Many gyms use a pre-exercise questionnaire (such as the PAR-Q+) to flag who should check with a doctor first. The assistant can suggest it; it doesn't interpret medical answers.

## Soreness vs. pain

Normal delayed-onset muscle soreness is a dull, spread-out ache in worked muscles, peaking a day or two after a new or harder session, and easing with gentle movement. Warning-sign pain is sharp, local, in a joint, or getting worse. Never advise training through pain.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the guideline minimum as the plan for someone training for an event, or as a target too high for a true beginner. Start where they are.
- Skipping strength work entirely; it's half the guideline.
- Reassuring someone about a symptom instead of sending them to a professional.
