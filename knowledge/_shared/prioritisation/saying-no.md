# Saying no

Every yes spends time that was already promised to something else. Saying no well protects the work you've committed to, and it's more helpful to the person asking than a yes you can't deliver.

## When to say no (or not yet)

Decline or defer when the request:

- doesn't serve a current objective or key result, and nobody with authority has made it a priority;
- would push committed work past its deadline;
- belongs to someone else's remit;
- is vague, with no clear goal or done-when. In that case ask for a brief instead (see [writing a task brief](../briefs-and-handoffs/writing-a-task-brief.md));
- needs approval, spend or access you don't have.

## How to say it

Keep it short and give the reason. A good no has three parts:

1. **The answer**, first. "I can't take this on this week."
2. **The trade-off**: what it would displace. "It would push the pricing scan past Thursday's meeting."
3. **What you can offer**: a later date, a smaller version, a pointer to the right owner, or a decision for someone above you.

```text
I can't take the webinar deck this week: it would push the pricing scan
past Thursday's leads meeting. I can do it from Monday, or a one-page
outline by Friday if that's enough. If the deck is more urgent than the
scan, can you check with the CEO and I'll switch?
```

Avoid both extremes. "Sorry, sorry, I'd love to but…" buries the answer. A bare "no" leaves the person stuck.

## Choosing your response

| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| Good idea, wrong time | Defer: give a date, or put it on the backlog with a note |
| Too big as asked | Offer a smaller version that fits |
| Someone else's job | Redirect to the named owner, and make the introduction |
| Conflicts with another priority | Escalate the trade-off to whoever owns both |
| Unclear request | Ask one clear question, or ask for a brief |
| Against policy or beyond your authority | Decline and say who can approve |

## Escalating a conflict

When two people with authority want conflicting things from you, don't pick a winner quietly. Put the trade-off to whoever can decide, in two or three sentences:

```text
Two asks collide this week: <A> for <person> (due <date>) and <B> for <person>
(due <date>). I can do one on time. My suggestion is <A> because <reason>.
Which should go first?
```

Then do what they decide, and log it in `/org/decisions.md` if it changes a commitment.

## Saying no to yourself

The same rule applies to your own ideas. Before you start unplanned work, ask whether it serves the done_when of a current task or a key result. If it doesn't, write it on the backlog with one line on why it might matter, and go back to the committed work.

## Checklist

- [ ] The answer comes first
- [ ] The trade-off is named
- [ ] An alternative is offered (a date, a smaller scope, the right owner)
- [ ] Conflicts escalated to someone who owns both, not settled silently
- [ ] Deferred items written down where they'll be found

## Common pitfalls

- **The silent no**: saying yes, then never doing it. That's worse than a clear no.
- **Over-explaining.** One reason is enough.
- **Saying no without the trade-off.** People take "no" personally and "not over X" practically.
- **Deferring forever.** If "later" never comes, say so and close the request.
- **Saying yes to look helpful,** then missing the committed deadline. The person you promised first pays for it.
