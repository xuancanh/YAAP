# Fair housing and screening

Discrimination claims often start with a careless listing or an inconsistent decision. A written, consistent process protects tenants and the landlord. This is general information; laws vary, and questions about a specific situation go to a lawyer or the fair housing agency.

## The law, in outline

- **US federal:** the Fair Housing Act bars discrimination in housing because of race or colour, religion, sex, national origin, familial status (households with children under 18, and pregnancy) and disability, according to the Department of Justice. It covers advertising, refusing to rent, different terms, steering and harassment. There are limited exemptions; don't assume one applies without advice.
- **US state and local laws** often add protected characteristics, such as source of income (including housing vouchers), age, marital status, sexual orientation or gender identity. Check the property's state and city.
- **Great Britain:** the Equality Act 2010 bars discrimination in letting and managing premises because of disability, race, religion or belief, sex, sexual orientation, gender reassignment, and pregnancy and maternity. (Age and marriage/civil partnership, protected elsewhere in the Act, are not covered by its premises provisions.) Check the current guidance; Northern Ireland has separate laws.
- **Elsewhere:** check the national equality law.

## Writing a neutral listing

Describe the property and the terms, not the ideal tenant.

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| "Perfect for a young professional" | "Close to the station; one-bedroom" |
| "No children" / "adults only" | Describe the size and layout |
| "Christian household" | (omit) |
| "No vouchers" / "no benefits" (illegal in some places) | State the rent and the screening criteria |
| "Must be able-bodied; no wheelchairs" | "Second floor, no lift" (a fact about the property) |

## Consistent screening

Write the criteria down before advertising, and apply them to every applicant in the same order:

1. Complete application.
2. Income or affordability test (state the rule, and how vouchers or other income are counted, per local law).
3. References from previous landlords.
4. Credit or background checks, where lawful and with consent. Some places limit use of criminal records or eviction history.
5. First qualified applicant gets the offer, or a stated selection method.

Record each applicant against the criteria and the reason for each decision. In England, landlords also have to check a tenant's right to rent; follow gov.uk's process and apply it to everyone, not only people who "seem foreign."

## Disability accommodation requests

Under the US Fair Housing Act, landlords must make reasonable accommodations (changes to rules) and allow reasonable modifications (physical changes, often at the tenant's cost) for people with disabilities.

Assistance animals are a common case. HUD's 2020 notice explains that:

- assistance animals aren't pets, so pet rules, pet fees and deposits generally don't apply to them;
- a landlord may ask for reliable documentation of the disability-related need when it isn't obvious;
- requests can be made at any time, including after moving in;
- decisions should be made promptly.

Handle every request in writing, calmly and quickly, and get advice before refusing one. Other countries have their own disability rules (in the UK, the Equality Act's reasonable adjustments duties).

## Common mistakes

- Telling one applicant the flat is taken and another that it's available.
- Different deposit amounts or rules for different applicants.
- Asking about plans for children, religion or origin in a viewing chat.
- Charging a pet fee for an assistance animal.
- Choosing the applicant you "clicked with" without written reasons.

## When to get advice

- Before refusing an accommodation request.
- If an applicant or tenant raises discrimination.
- Before using any criteria involving criminal records, income source or immigration status.

US: a local fair housing organisation or HUD's Office of Fair Housing; UK: a solicitor, landlord association or the Equality Advisory and Support Service.
