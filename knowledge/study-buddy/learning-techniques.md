# Learning techniques that work

A large 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues rated ten common study techniques. Two came out with high utility across learners, subjects and test types: **practice testing** and **distributed (spaced) practice**. Popular habits such as rereading, highlighting and summarising were rated low. The Learning Scientists, a group of cognitive psychologists, teach a similar set of six strategies.

## The high-value techniques

**Retrieval practice (practice testing).** Pull information out of memory instead of putting it back in. Flashcards, practice questions, closing the book and writing what you remember, explaining it aloud from memory.
*Apply:* end every study session with a short quiz; start the next session by recalling the last one.

**Spacing.** Spread study of a topic over days and weeks instead of one long block. Forgetting a little between sessions makes the next recall harder and the memory stronger.
*Apply:* three one-hour sessions over a week beat one three-hour session. Spaced repetition decks automate this.

**Interleaving.** Mix problem types in one session (A, B, C, B, A, C) instead of blocks (AAA, BBB). It's harder and feels slower, but it teaches you to choose the method, which is what the exam asks. Rated moderate in the review; strongest for maths and problem-solving.
*Apply:* once a topic is basically understood, practise it mixed with earlier topics.

**Elaboration.** Ask how and why; connect new ideas to what you know. "Why does this step work? How is this like the last topic?"

**Concrete examples.** Abstract ideas stick when attached to several varied examples. Ask for two examples and one non-example.

**Dual coding.** Combine words with visuals: a diagram, timeline or flowchart drawn from memory, then checked.

## Feels good, does less

| Habit | Why it's weak | Swap for |
|---|---|---|
| Rereading notes | Familiarity feels like knowing | Close the notes and recall |
| Highlighting | Passive; you mark more than you learn | Turn highlights into questions |
| Copying notes neatly | Effort goes into presentation | Rewrite from memory, then fill gaps |
| Cramming the night before | Works briefly, fades fast | Spaced sessions over weeks |
| Watching solved examples only | Recognition, not production | Do a similar problem without looking |

## Why it feels backwards

Effective methods often feel harder and slower, and learners judge them as worse. Researchers call these "desirable difficulties". Tell the user this up front, so a struggling quiz reads as progress rather than failure.

## Worked examples for new material

For a brand-new type of problem, studying a fully worked example first and then attempting a similar one is usually better than struggling alone from the start. Fade the support: full example → partly worked (fill in the last steps) → solve alone.

## Applying it in a session (60 minutes)

1. **5 min:** recall last session's key points without notes; check.
2. **25 min:** new material, with self-explanation ("why does this work?").
3. **20 min:** mixed practice questions, including older topics.
4. **10 min:** check errors; turn each mistake into a flashcard or a note in the error log.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a high score on a quiz taken straight after reading as proof of learning. Test again days later.
- Only quizzing on what's already known.
- Abandoning spacing in exam month in favour of marathon rereading.
